## Passage 1 (Questions 1–4)

Liberal accounts of civil disobedience usually list its conditions as if they were items on a checklist: the act must be illegal, conscientious, non-violent and public, and the person who commits it must be willing to accept the legal penalty. Of these, publicity tends to receive the least attention, as though it were merely a matter of not sneaking about. Yet it is publicity that does most of the theoretical work. A secret violation of the law, however principled, addresses no one; it is a private refusal. A public violation, by contrast, is a form of speech directed at the majority—an appeal to principles that the disobedient claims the majority already holds but has failed to apply. The other conditions follow from this. Non-violence matters because one does not persuade a fellow citizen by injuring him; willingness to accept punishment matters because it shows that the appeal is made from within the shared order rather than against it.

Critics have long objected that this last requirement is servile. Why, they ask, should a person who is right about an unjust law also submit to the penalty for breaking it? The objection has force against a moralising version of the theory, in which punishment is accepted as a kind of penance. It has less force against the version sketched here, in which accepting the penalty is not an admission of guilt but a credential: evidence, offered to sceptical onlookers, that the act is not simply self-interested law-breaking wearing the costume of principle.

What has changed is the economy in which such credentials circulate. When the means of making an act public were scarce—when it required a crowd, a newspaper willing to report, a courtroom to supply a stage—publicity was itself costly, and its cost did some of the work of distinguishing the conscientious from the opportunistic. Today almost any act can be made public at negligible expense, and the publicity of an act therefore tells observers very little about the seriousness of the person who performs it. The burden of signalling has shifted almost entirely onto the willingness to pay a price.

This shift has an awkward consequence. If the price is what persuades, there is a temptation to seek it out—to stage arrests and court penalties because they confer credibility—and the disobedient act risks becoming a performance of sacrifice whose audience is impressed by the suffering rather than moved by the argument. That would invert the original logic, in which the penalty mattered only as a guarantee that an argument was being made in good faith. The appeal to shared principles, the content of the address, would become secondary to the spectacle of its cost.

The remedy is not to abandon any of the traditional conditions but to remember their order of dependence. A sacrifice that carries no intelligible appeal to the majority's own commitments is not civil disobedience made more convincing; it is something else, however admirable, that has borrowed the name.

## Passage 2 (Questions 5–8)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

Histories of insurance often begin with mathematics. In the standard telling, the calculus of probability, worked out by a handful of seventeenth-century thinkers, made it possible for the first time to put a price on uncertainty, and the insurance industry followed as a practical application of the new science. The story has the appeal of a clean lineage, but it runs into an awkward chronological fact. Marine insurance was a thriving business in the trading cities of Italy some three centuries before anyone had formalised the mathematics that was supposedly its precondition.

What made it possible was not a theory of chance but a change in the form of a contract. Earlier merchants had managed the hazards of sea trade chiefly through the sea loan, under which a lender advanced money for a voyage and forfeited repayment if the ship was lost. The arrangement bundled together two quite different things: credit, for the merchant who needed capital, and protection, for the merchant who feared loss. It also attracted the suspicion of authorities who saw in the high rates charged on such loans a disguised form of usury. The premium contract dissolved the bundle. A merchant who had capital of his own could now buy protection alone, paying a fixed sum in advance to an underwriter who promised compensation in the event of loss; no loan, and therefore no interest, was involved at all.

How were such premiums set without probability theory? Largely by judgement, and the judgement drew on information that no formula could have captured: the reputation of the captain, the condition of the vessel, the season, rumours of pirates or war, the price other underwriters had already accepted. Underwriters rarely bore a whole risk themselves; they took a share, and their willingness to do so was itself information to those deciding whether to take the next. An underwriter known to be careful could, simply by signing first, make a risk easier to place. Pricing was thus social before it was statistical. It depended on dense networks in which news travelled quickly and reputations could be lost in an afternoon.

Nor did mathematics, when it arrived, transform marine underwriting as thoroughly as the standard story implies. Probability theory is most powerful when risks are numerous and alike, so that individual uncertainties average out over a large pool. Human lives approximate that condition well enough for actuarial tables to be useful. Voyages do not. Each combined a particular ship, crew, cargo, route and season, and the number of voyages that were genuinely comparable was small. For a long time the best guide to a voyage's danger remained the informed opinion of people who knew the sea, the ships and the men who sailed them.

The point is not that mathematics was irrelevant to insurance, but that the institution was invented by people who had no need of it. What they needed—and built—was a way of separating risk from credit, and a market in which judgements about risk could be shared, tested and priced.

## Passage 3 (Questions 9–12)

Disputes about punctuation are conducted with a fervour that would be surprising if the stakes were only typographical. A misplaced apostrophe on a shop sign can provoke letters to newspapers; arguments about the final comma in a list have divided editors for generations. The intensity suggests that people take punctuation rules to have the same kind of authority as rules of grammar, so that to break one is to make a mistake rather than a choice. The history of the marks themselves suggests otherwise.

For most of their existence, punctuation marks were not rules at all but aids to performance. Texts were read aloud, and the marks told a reader where to breathe, where to pause briefly and where at length. They were added, often inconsistently, by scribes, readers and later printers, and a single manuscript might carry the marks of several hands, each guiding a different reading. Readers who disagreed about the sense of a passage could, quite literally, mark it differently. Only gradually, as silent reading became the norm, did the marks come to be understood as indicators of grammatical structure—signals not of where the voice should rest but of how the clauses of a sentence relate to one another.

The two functions have never fully separated, and much of the heat in modern disputes comes from treating one as if it were the other. A comma placed where a speaker would pause may be condemned as grammatically unjustified; a comma demanded by grammatical analysis may be resisted because it chops up a sentence that reads smoothly without it. Each side appeals to a standard the other does not recognise, and each concludes that the other simply does not know the rules.

It would be more accurate to say that there are no rules of the kind either side imagines. Grammar, in the linguist's sense, describes regularities that speakers follow whether or not they have been taught them; an English speaker who has never opened a style guide does not put the article after the noun. Punctuation enjoys no such grounding. It is a technology of the page, learned rather than acquired, and its conventions are best understood as solutions to a practical problem: how to stop a reader from misconstruing a sentence. The useful question to ask of a comma is therefore not whether it is correct but whether it forestalls a likely misreading at a tolerable cost in fluency.

This is not a licence for anarchy. Conventions have a value that does not depend on the merits of any particular convention, much as the value of driving on one side of the road does not depend on the superiority of that side. A writer who punctuates by personal judgement alone, however sensible each choice, forces readers to learn a private system—and no reader can be expected to master a new one with every book. That cost may exceed whatever clarity the system gains. The case against treating punctuation as grammar is not a case for treating it as self-expression.

## Passage 4 (Questions 13–16)

For several decades, the case for culture in animals rested on a method of elimination. Researchers compiled the behaviours of different populations of a species—how they cracked nuts, groomed one another, used leaves to collect water—and identified those that were common in some places and absent in others. Where a difference could not be explained by the local environment or by genetic differences between populations, it was attributed to social learning: the animals did things differently because they had learned from different neighbours.

The method was always vulnerable. An absence of ecological explanation is not the same as an absence of ecological cause, and critics were quick to point out that a behaviour found in one forest but not another might reflect some feature of the habitat that researchers had simply failed to measure. Each new survey that failed to find an ecological difference strengthened the case only a little, since another unmeasured variable was always available. The inference to culture was, in effect, an inference from ignorance: whatever was left over once the known explanations had been subtracted.

More recent work has tried to observe transmission directly. In one kind of experiment, a few individuals in a wild population are trained to solve a feeding puzzle in one of two ways and then released back into their groups. If the trained solution spreads, and groups seeded with different solutions end up doing things differently, the role of social learning is no longer inferred from what is left over; it is watched as it happens. Such experiments have shown that some birds not only copy a technique but come to prefer the local variant even after discovering the alternative, which looks a good deal like conformity to a tradition.

It would be a mistake, however, to treat these results as settling the older argument. An experiment demonstrates that a population can sustain a tradition; it does not show that the differences observed between wild populations arose that way. The two methods answer different questions, and the evidence from one does not automatically transfer to the other. What the experiments do accomplish is to remove the objection, made in advance of any evidence, that animals could not maintain arbitrary local behaviours by social means. Once that possibility is established, the burden shifts: it becomes reasonable to ask critics what unmeasured ecological factor they have in mind.

Whether any of this amounts to culture depends on what is required of the word. If culture means behaviour transmitted socially and maintained as a local tradition, the case is now strong for a number of species, among them birds, whales and several primates. If it means a tradition that accumulates—each generation building on the modifications of the last, so that the result exceeds what any individual could invent alone—the evidence is far thinner. That is the more interesting question, and the honest answer is that we do not yet know how unusual our own species is.
