# CAT 2026 VARC — MOCK 2: ANSWER KEY AND SOLUTIONS

**Purpose:** practise the reasoning patterns seen in CAT 2020. All the material is new; nothing is copied from the 2020 papers.

**Format:** 24 questions, 40 minutes. There are 19 MCQs and 5 TITA questions.
- **Verbal Ability:** 3 para jumbles, 2 odd-sentence-out and 3 para summaries. This is the exact CAT 2020 VA mix.
- **Reading Comprehension:** 4 passages × 4 questions, the modern 24-question layout.

| Wrong MCQ | −1 |
| Wrong TITA | 0 |

## Passage 1: Civil disobedience and publicity (political philosophy; difficult)

**Map of the argument:**

| Paragraph | Point |
|---|---|
| P1 | Publicity does the theoretical work. Disobedience is an address to the majority, an appeal to principles the majority already holds. The other conditions follow from this. |
| P2 | Critics call accepting punishment servile. The author replies that accepting the penalty is a *credential*, not a *penance*. |
| P3 | Publicity is now cheap, so the job of signalling has moved to the willingness to pay a price. |
| P4 | The risk: price-seeking turns disobedience into a spectacle of sacrifice. |
| P5 | The remedy is to keep all the conditions but remember their order of dependence: the appeal comes first. |

### Q1. Answer: B. Counterfactual (very difficult)

**How to work this type:** negate each option. The answer is the option whose *negation* supports the author's account. Here, the account under test is the one about why accepting punishment matters.

The author's account: accepting the penalty "is not an admission of guilt but a credential". It is evidence that the appeal is made in good faith from within the shared order.

**Option by option:**
- **(B) correct.** Negated: "accepting the penalty does *not* amount to conceding that one acted wrongly." That is exactly the author's reply to the critics in P2.
- **(A) wrong.** Negated: "publicity was never more costly than now." This contradicts P3. It weakens the author's story about how signalling shifted, so it does not support anything.
- **(C) wrong.** Negated: "onlookers are not inclined to suspect self-interest." The credential is offered to sceptical onlookers. Without scepticism the credential has no job, so the negation undercuts the account.
- **(D) wrong.** Negated: "an appeal can be made by injuring people." This undercuts the P1 rationale for non-violence. It is also about the wrong condition.

**Takeaway:** write out the negation of every option before judging any of them. The trap is choosing the option that is *true* according to the passage. C and D are both true, but their negations weaken the account.

### Q2. Answer: D. Author would recommend (difficult)

**Evidence:** "The remedy is not to abandon any of the traditional conditions but to remember their order of dependence." A sacrifice without "an intelligible appeal to the majority's own commitments" is not civil disobedience.

D puts the appeal first and keeps the penalty as a supporting credential, which is the author's order.

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(A)** It sounds like P1 ("publicity does most of the theoretical work"). But P3 says publicity is now cheap and "tells observers very little". Maximising coverage is not the remedy. This is a scope trap: true of the theory, wrong as advice for today.
- **(B)** This is the temptation the author warns against in P4 ("seek it out… court penalties"). It reverses the author's advice. It is tempting because P3 really does say the burden "has shifted almost entirely onto the willingness to pay a price".
- **(C)** This is the critics' view (servility), which the author rejects.

### Q3. Answer: B. Keyword set (difficult)

**What runs through the whole passage:**
- **Disobedience and publicity:** P1, P3.
- **Credential:** the penalty as credential (P2), the signalling problem (P3), and "confer credibility" (P4).
- **Appeal:** P1 and the remedy in P5.

**Why the other sets fail:**
- **(A)** "Sacrifice" and "spectacle" belong only to the P4 warning, and "media" is a P3 detail. This is the part-for-whole trap; CAT 2020's anarchism keyword question used the same mechanism. A also leaves out "appeal", which is both the foundation (P1) and the remedy (P5).
- **(C)** "Servility" and "penance" come only from the critics' objection in P2.
- **(D)** "Newspapers" and "opportunism" are details from P3. D also drops disobedience itself.

**Takeaway:** for a keyword-set question, each word must recur across the passage. If a word belongs to a single paragraph, it doesn't belong in the set.

### Q4. Answer: A. Implication across paragraphs (difficult)

**The pieces you need:**
- P1: the other conditions "follow from" publicity understood as an address.
- P4: "the penalty mattered only as a guarantee that an argument was being made in good faith".
- P5: "order of dependence".

Put together, the penalty depends on the appeal for its value, not the other way round. That is A.

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(B)** A plausible remedy the author never proposes. The author keeps the conditions as they are and reorders our understanding of them.
- **(C)** The wrong ordering principle. The order is about logical dependence, not about how much each condition demands of the person.
- **(D) Misattribution.** P1 makes publicity, understood as address, the foundation. Non-violence is derived from it.

## Passage 2: The origins of marine insurance (economic history; moderate)

**Map of the argument:**

| Paragraph | Point |
|---|---|
| P1 | The standard story is that mathematics came first. The chronology refutes it: insurance existed before the mathematics. |
| P2 | The real innovation was a contract. The premium contract separated protection from credit, which the sea loan had bundled together. |
| P3 | Premiums were priced by social judgement and shared information. |
| P4 | Probability theory has limits with heterogeneous voyages. |
| P5 | Mathematics was not irrelevant, but the institution did not need it at its invention. |

### Q5. Answer: C. EXCEPT (moderate)

**Why C is the exception:** protection without debt describes the *premium contract*, not the sea loan. This is a misattribution, and it is the only option that misdescribes the sea loan.

**The three options the passage supports:**
- **(A)** It "bundled together… credit… and protection".
- **(B)** "Suspicion… a disguised form of usury".
- **(D)** The lender "forfeited repayment if the ship was lost": the borrowed sum was lost to the lender.

### Q6. Answer: A. Phrase in context (moderate)

**Evidence:** "The arrangement bundled together two quite different things: credit… and protection." "The bundle" is therefore that pairing, held within one contract.

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(B) Close distractor.** It pairs the loan with its *interest*. That is the usury concern, a separate point raised in the next sentence.
- **(C)** Hazards are listed in P3 as inputs to pricing, not as what was bundled.
- **(D)** Shared underwriting comes from P3. It is a different kind of "bundling" (syndication) that the passage never calls a bundle.

**Takeaway:** resolve a pointer phrase like "the bundle" back to its antecedent sentence. Don't resolve it to the nearest topic.

### Q7. Answer: C. Analogy (difficult)

**The limitation in P4:** probability works when risks are "numerous and alike". Voyages were few and each was unique, so informed experts outperformed the tables.

**Matching each option to that structure:**

| Option | Cases few and unique? | Experts outperform statistics? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(C)** artworks | Yes, one-of-a-kind | Yes, specialists' appraisals | **Fits** |
| (A) casino | No, a large pool | Statistics work | Shows probability's strength |
| (D) bank loans | No, a large pool | Statistics work (and are cheap) | Shows probability's strength |
| (B) doctor | Not stated | Intuition used | Wrong reason |

**Why B is the strongest trap:** the doctor uses intuition because studies "have never been carried out", a contingent gap that research could fill. The voyages resisted statistics *by their nature*: genuinely comparable cases were few.

**Takeaway:** an analogy must match the *reason* for the outcome, not only the outcome.

### Q8. Answer: C. Primary purpose (moderate)

**Evidence:** "the institution was invented by people who had no need of it. What they needed—and built—was a way of separating risk from credit, and a market…"

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(A) Strongest rival: true, but only part of the passage.** P3 and P4 do say that pricing was social and stayed largely judgemental. But A leaves out the passage's central claim about origins: the premium contract that separated risk from credit (P2), restated in P5. A covers paragraphs 3–4, not the whole.
- **(B) Extreme.** "The point is not that mathematics was irrelevant."
- **(D) Part for whole.** Usury suspicion is one background factor in P2. The passage never says it caused the premium contract.

## Passage 3: The authority of punctuation (language; moderate)

**Map of the argument:**

| Paragraph | Point |
|---|---|
| P1 | People treat punctuation rules as if they had the authority of grammar. |
| P2 | Historically, punctuation marks were aids to reading aloud, and only later became grammatical signals. |
| P3 | Modern disputes confuse these two functions. |
| P4 | There are no rules of the grammatical kind. Punctuation is a learned technology for preventing misreading, so the test is misreading prevented against fluency lost. |
| P5 | Conventions still have value as shared conventions. Idiosyncrasy costs the reader. |

### Q9. Answer: D. Contrast (moderate)

**Evidence:** grammar "describes regularities that speakers follow whether or not they have been taught them"; punctuation is "learned rather than acquired".

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(A) Reversal of the history.** The marks moved *from* rhythm *to* grammatical structure.
- **(B) Fusion or overgeneralisation.** Preventing misreading is the purpose of *punctuation* conventions only. Grammar is described as regularities, not conventions.
- **(C)** A neat symmetry the passage never draws. It isn't claimed that grammar governs speech only.

### Q10. Answer: A. Author would advise (moderate)

**Evidence:**
- P4: "whether it forestalls a likely misreading at a tolerable cost in fluency".
- P5: conventions have value, and a writer who uses "personal judgement alone" imposes costs.

A combines both points. It is phrased differently from the passage ("spares readers a plausible misunderstanding" = "forestalls a likely misreading"; "clumsy" = cost "in fluency").

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(B)** The breath-pause side of the dispute. Only one of the two standards the author criticises.
- **(C)** The grammatical side of the dispute. The other of the two standards.
- **(D)** "Anarchy". P5 explicitly rejects it.

### Q11. Answer: B. LEAST likely to agree (moderate)

**Why B is the answer:** B treats a departure from the rule as a *mistake* even when no misreading results. The author denies "rules of the kind either side imagines" and replaces "correct?" with "does it forestall misreading?". So the author would least agree with B.

**The three options the author would accept:**
- **(A)** P3: "Each side appeals to a standard the other does not recognise."
- **(C)** P2: marks were "added… by scribes, readers and later printers".
- **(D)** P5: "however sensible each choice… that cost may exceed…"

**Trap:** a hasty reader may reason that because the author values convention, the author would call departures mistakes. But in the author's view the value of convention lies in *cost to the reader*. It does not make a departure an error.

### Q12. Answer: D. Weaken (difficult)

**The target claim:** grammar describes regularities "speakers follow whether or not they have been taught them", while punctuation "enjoys no such grounding… learned rather than acquired".

**Why D:** if untaught people *reliably* mark the same divisions in unpunctuated text, there is an untaught regularity behind punctuation. That is the very kind of grounding the author denies. D goes to the heart of the "learned rather than acquired" contrast.

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(A)** Continued disagreement between style guides fits the author's picture of convention without a deeper basis. It supports the claim.
- **(B) Strongest trap.** It shows that grammatically motivated commas reduce misreading. But that is the author's own *practical* account of punctuation, as a tool against misconstrual, and says nothing about untaught grounding. B is compatible with the claim.
- **(C)** Standardisation by printers is a learned, institutional development. It supports "technology of the page".

**Takeaway:** identify exactly what is being denied, here *untaught* grounding. The best underminer supplies evidence of that specific thing, not merely evidence that punctuation is useful.

## Passage 4: Culture in animals (animal behaviour; moderate–difficult)

**Map of the argument:**

| Paragraph | Point |
|---|---|
| P1 | The method of elimination. |
| P2 | The critics' objection: an unmeasured feature of the habitat could explain the difference (an "inference from ignorance"). New surveys help only a little. |
| P3 | Seeding experiments let researchers watch transmission happen, including conformity. |
| P4 | These experiments do not settle the question about variation in the wild, but they remove the objection in principle and shift the burden onto the critics. |
| P5 | Culture as tradition: strong evidence. Culture as cumulative: thin evidence. The question of human uniqueness is open. |

### Q13. Answer: A. Weaken (difficult)

**The objection:** a behaviour that differs between groups "might reflect some feature of the habitat that researchers had simply failed to measure".

**Why A:**
- The groups share *one habitat* and an ancestry, so no unmeasured habitat feature can separate them.
- The individual who switches groups adopts its new group's method, which shows the method is learned socially.
- A targets the objection *for this behaviour*, as the question stem requires.

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(B) Strong trap.** Measuring more variables still leaves "another unmeasured variable… always available". The passage says such surveys strengthen the case "only a little".
- **(C)** A seeding experiment. P4 says experimental evidence "does not automatically transfer" to the differences seen in the wild.
- **(D)** Rules out genetics only. The critics' objection is ecological.

**Takeaway:** a good weakener closes the specific gap the objection points to. Here that gap is an unmeasured habitat difference.

### Q14. Answer: D. Inference about the author's view (moderate)

**Evidence:** the experiments "remove the objection, made in advance of any evidence"; they do "not show that the differences observed between wild populations arose that way".

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(A)** Contradicted: "a mistake… to treat these results as settling the older argument".
- **(B)** Unsupported comparison. The passage never ranks the two methods' informativeness, and never criticises the use of trained animals.
- **(C)** Contradicted: this is exactly what the experiments do *not* show.

### Q15. Answer: B. Application to a concept (difficult)

**The demanding sense of culture:** a tradition "that accumulates… each generation building on the modifications of the last, so that the result exceeds what any individual could invent alone".

**Why B:** it shows refinement over generations *and* a result that naive individuals cannot reach alone. Both features of the definition are present.

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(A) Strongest trap.** This is conformity to a local variant, which the passage cites in P3 as evidence of *tradition*. That is the weaker sense of culture.
- **(C)** The method-of-elimination pattern, which bears on the weaker sense at most.
- **(D)** Rapid spread is transmission, not accumulation.

### Q16. Answer: C. Author's attitude (moderate)

**Evidence:** "the evidence is far thinner… we do not yet know how unusual our own species is." The stem asks about culture *in the cumulative sense*. In the weaker sense, the author is not undecided: other species clearly have it.

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(A) Overreach.** "Far thinner" is not the same as "none".
- **(B) Half-right.** The answer does depend on definition, but the author calls the cumulative question "the more interesting question". That is not doubt about whether the question means anything.
- **(D) Extreme.** The author says culture in the weaker sense only is "strong for a number of species".

## Verbal Ability

### Q17. Answer: 2413. Para jumble (moderate)

**The sequence:**
1. **2** opens. Rubbish heaps are rich records; "the heap" in 1 needs this.
2. **4** contrasts discards with grave goods: "never meant to be seen".
3. **1** "this indifference to posterity" refers to 4's "never meant to be seen". This is the heap's value: habit, not aspiration.
4. **3** "*Hence*" draws the consequence of 1's habit/aspiration distinction: graves preserve remembrance, middens preserve diet.

**Alternatives tested:**
- **2-3-4-1:** "Hence" right after 2 has nothing to follow from. A heap being the richest record does not imply that a grave records remembrance. The order fails.
- **2-4-3-1:** "Hence" after 4 is possible. But then "this indifference" in 1 points back past 3, which says nothing about indifference. Inferior.
- **4-2-…:** 1 then needs "the heap" and "indifference" right behind it. No ordering allows that.

*Audit note:* the first draft of sentence 3 lacked "Hence", and the blind audit found 2341 equally coherent. Adding "Hence" fixed that by tying 3 to 1.

### Q18. Answer: 3142. Para jumble (moderate)

**The sequence:**
1. **3** sets out the puzzle: why do some cartels last?
2. **1** explains why lasting is puzzling. "Them" refers to cartels.
3. **4** supplies the reason, signalled by "after all": each member's incentive to cheat.
4. **2** resolves the puzzle: "The durable ones" share a monitoring feature.

**Alternatives tested:**
- 1 cannot open, because "them" has no referent.
- 2 placed right after 3 answers the puzzle before the paragraph has explained why it is a puzzle, and leaves 1 and 4 stranded.

### Q19. Answer: 1432. Para jumble (difficult)

**The sequence:**
1. **1** gives the usual explanation: wordplay.
2. **4** "Yet" brings in a counterexample: jokes with no puns still fail.
3. **3** "What they depend on instead" is the real dependence, unstated shared assumptions. "Instead" contrasts with wordplay.
4. **2** "Because none of this is on the page" refers to 3's assumptions "without stating". It gives the consequence for translators.

**Alternatives tested:**
- **1-2-4-3:** in this position "none of this" would refer to wordplay. But wordplay *is* on the page, so 2 cannot follow 1.
- **1-4-2-3:** 2's "none of this" would point at puns that 4 has just said are absent, so the reference doesn't work.

The unique anchor is the link between 3's "without stating" and 2's "none of this is on the page".

### Q20. Answer: 3. Odd sentence out (moderate)

**The coherent paragraph is 4-1-5-2:**
1. **4** states the benefits.
2. **1** "These benefits" accrue where trees already are.
3. **5** "And trees already are" in neighbourhoods that were wealthy.
4. **2** "thus" concludes: canopy maps past income.

**Why 3 is odd:** it stays on the topic of urban trees and even uses the paragraph's benefit/cost framing. But the paragraph's argument is about how *benefits are distributed*. Sentence 3 introduces costs to municipal budgets, which the chain never takes up.

It also cannot be inserted:
- Placed after 4, it would make "These benefits" in 1 refer to costs.
- Placed after 2, it would be a new argument tacked onto a conclusion.

You find the odd sentence by tracing the logical chain, not by spotting a topic shift.

### Q21. Answer: 2. Odd sentence out (difficult)

**The coherent paragraph is 3-5-1-4:**
1. **3** states the expectation that the game would lose its appeal.
2. **5** "The opposite happened."
3. **1** "The reason" explains the paradox: engines became instruments.
4. **4** illustrates how masters use engines.

**Why 2 is odd:** it is tightly linked to the *wording* of 3 ("a solved puzzle"), which is the trap. But it corrects a technical premise rather than advancing the argument about why interest rose.

It cannot be inserted:
- After 3, it would separate the expectation from "The opposite happened".
- Anywhere else, it interrupts the reason–illustration sequence.

**Takeaway:** a sentence can share key words with the paragraph and still be odd. Ask whether it advances the paragraph's claim.

### Q22. Answer: A. Para summary (moderate)

**Why A:** it keeps both halves. Memory is weak as evidence of events, and its distortions are themselves evidence of meaning.

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(B) Reversal.** The distortions are *data*, not errors to be corrected.
- **(C) Overgeneralisation.** The paragraph says testimony is evidence of meaning *and its revision*. It never claims testimony is "more about the present than the past". This is the closest trap.
- **(D)** A role the paragraph doesn't give. It says written records are *better* for events.

### Q23. Answer: D. Para summary (moderate)

**Why D:** it keeps the bargain, the condition (disclosure must be useful), the two pieces of evidence (drafting practice and engineers' habits) and the hedged conclusion ("may be receiving… less").

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(A)** An extreme prescription. The paragraph gives no remedy.
- **(B) Causal distortion.** Engineers read patents *only* to check for infringement. They don't avoid reading them because reading creates infringement exposure.
- **(C) Wrong cause.** Engineers' habits are *evidence* of low usefulness. The paragraph locates the cause in drafting ("claim as much… revealing as little").

### Q24. Answer: B. Para summary (easy)

**Why B:** it captures the change from passive protection to active, selective strengthening.

**Why the other options fail:**
- **(A)** Keeps the old view that the paragraph revises.
- **(C) Scope shift.** Cues strengthen material that was *already learned*. That is not new learning during sleep.
- **(D)** "All of the day's memories" contradicts "selective".

## Diagnostic guide

| If you missed… | Work on |
|---|---|
| Q1 | Negating every option before evaluating "if false" questions |
| Q2, Q10 | Separating the author's remedy from the views the author describes or criticises |
| Q3 | Keyword sets: does each word recur across the whole passage? |
| Q7, Q15 | Matching the *reason* in analogies and definitions, not the surface outcome |
| Q13 | Weakeners that close the specific gap an objection points to |
| Q19, Q21 | Anchoring on what "this", "none of this" and "the opposite" refer to |
| Q22, Q23 | Summary traps that misplace causes or overgeneralise |

**Pacing:** Passage 1 and Q1 are the main time sinks. In a real CAT, a passage this dense is a candidate to skip or leave until last. Passages 2 and 3 offer the quickest marks.
