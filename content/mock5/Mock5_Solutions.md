# CAT 2026 VARC — MOCK 5: ANSWER KEY AND SOLUTIONS

**Purpose:** a realistic exam-day simulation. It is meant to feel like a plausible CAT, not to be the hardest paper in the set.

**Format:**
- 24 questions in 40 minutes: 22 MCQ and 2 TITA.
- The Verbal Ability questions follow the CAT 2024 mix: no para jumbles, 2 odd sentence out, 3 summaries and 3 sentence placements.
- Reading Comprehension and Verbal Ability questions are interleaved, as IMS reported for CAT 2024.

**Marking:**

| Outcome | Marks |
|---|---|
| Correct answer | +3 |
| Wrong MCQ | −1 |
| Wrong TITA | 0 |

**Design choices behind the exam-day feel:**

| Design choice | How it shows up |
|---|---|
| Passage difficulty does not rise steadily through the paper | The hardest passage (law, Passage 1) comes first and the easiest (ecology, Passage 4) comes last |
| Verbal Ability is spread through the paper, not saved for the end | Questions 1, 2, 7, 12, 13, 18, 23 and 24 sit between the passages |
| Some question types are less familiar | Q4 is an odd-pair-out question; Q10 asks about a hypothetical change to a system; Q16 interprets a figurative phrase |
| Passage voices vary | Passage 3 is written as a book review |

**Difficulty labels** are expert judgement and have not been validated against real candidates' responses.

## Strategy note

The four passages differ a lot in difficulty, so the order in which you attack them matters.

| Passage | Questions | Topic | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3–6 | Precedent in law | Dense and abstract; the hardest |
| 2 | 8–11 | Recommendation algorithms and taste | Moderate |
| 3 | 14–17 | Book review on public health | Moderate |
| 4 | 19–22 | Rewilding and baselines | The easiest |

- **Read Passage 4 even if time runs short.** Its four questions are the cheapest marks in the paper.
- **Passage 1 is the passage to consider skipping.** A strong candidate might take it last or attempt only Q3 and Q5.
- **The two TITA questions (Q7, Q18) carry no negative marking.** Always enter an answer.

## Verbal Ability, Questions 1–2

### Q1. Answer: B (summary; easy)

**Why B is right:** It captures the mechanism, a price of zero removing the moment of deliberation. It also keeps the conclusion that the cost is moved, not removed.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Contradicted: the paragraph says it is "not because delivery has become costless".
- **(C)** Gives an invented cause. Nothing is said about customers overestimating costs.
- **(D)** Contradicted: "less a discount", and the cost is recovered through prices.

### Q2. Answer: C, gap 3 (sentence placement; moderate)

**What the sentence needs:** "*Even then*, few people expected the requirement to outlast the conflict." So it needs an introduced requirement and a conflict, "then" must refer to a time, and it should come before the surprise that the requirement survived.

| Gap | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1 | No requirement or conflict has been introduced yet. |
| 2 | Still describes the old letters of safe conduct, so "the requirement" has no referent. |
| **3** | **Correct.** It follows the wartime demand and sets up the next sentence, "*Yet* measures introduced as temporary… survived the peace." |
| 4 | Locally plausible, because the topic is still the requirement. But the requirement has already outlasted the war, so a sentence saying few expected it to outlast the conflict comes too late. |

## Passage 1: Precedent (law; hardest)

**How the passage is built:**
1. **The puzzle.** If a precedent was right, the court would reach the same result anyway; if wrong, following it repeats the error.
2. **The standard defences.** Reliance, treating like cases alike, efficiency and constraint on judges.
3. **The author's thesis.** These defences vary by domain. Precedent should be strongest where being settled is the main good, and weakest where the error itself endures, as with basic liberties.
4. **Distinguishing.** Courts use it instead of explicit overruling. In moderation it is reasonable; unrestrained, it produces a thicket.
5. **The irony.** Distinguishing can destroy the predictability it was meant to protect.

### Q3. Answer: A (principle the author proposes; difficult)

**Evidence:** "The strength of precedent should therefore vary with the kind of question it settles—strongest where settledness is the main good, weakest where it is the error itself that endures."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B)** This is the strongest rival. It treats institutional adjustment as the measure, but the author explicitly rejects that for basic liberties: "not made more tolerable by the fact that institutions have grown accustomed to it."
- **(C)** Court hierarchy is never discussed.
- **(D)** Factual similarity is what *distinguishing* turns on. It is not the author's criterion for strength.

### Q4. Answer: B (odd pair out; difficult)

**How to read the question:** check each pair. Does the author say this consideration applies *with special force* to this kind of rule?

| Pair | Does the author support it? |
|---|---|
| (A) Reliance and land transfer | Yes: "Reliance matters most… on the rules governing… the transfer of land" |
| **(B) Institutions' reliance and precedents misreading a basic liberty** | **No.** The author says the opposite: such a precedent "is not made more tolerable by the fact that institutions have grown accustomed to it" |
| (C) The cost of an enduring error and rules whose errors fall on people with no say | Yes: reliance "matters least… where the cost of an error falls on people who had no say in it". There, precedent is "weakest", because "the error itself… endures" |
| (D) Settledness and commercial rules | Yes: there "the content of the rule often matters less than its being settled" |

**Takeaway:** in pairing questions, check both halves of every pair. The odd pair often reuses a word from a correct pair (here, "reliance") and attaches it to the wrong domain.

### Q5. Answer: C (inference about the author's attitude; moderate)

**Evidence:** "in moderation it is a reasonable way of testing an old rule… Used without restraint, it produces a different problem."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Calling distinguishing "dishonest" goes too far. The author calls it "reasonable" in moderation, and preserving "the appearance of continuity" is a description, not an accusation.
- **(B)** Reverses the irony in the last paragraph.
- **(D)** The author never prescribes explicit overruling "whenever a precedent is doubted".

### Q6. Answer: B (weaken; difficult)

**The argument being weakened:** reliance "matters least where no one could reasonably have planned around the rule". The example given is precedents on basic liberties.

**Why B weakens it:** if people routinely plan around precedents on basic liberties and lose a great deal when those precedents are overturned, then reliance *does* matter there. That undercuts the premise behind varying precedent's strength.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Supports the paragraph's point about commercial rules.
- **(C)** How often such precedents are overturned is a descriptive fact. It says nothing about how strong they *should* be.
- **(D)** This is the closest rival. If judges disagree about what counts as a basic liberty, the principle becomes harder to *apply*, but its premise is untouched. B attacks the premise directly, so it weakens the argument more.

## Verbal Ability, Question 7

### Q7. Answer: 2 (odd sentence out; moderate)

**The coherent paragraph is 3-5-1-4:**
1. **Sentence 3:** cargo was once handled by hand.
2. **Sentence 5:** a ship could spend as long in port as at sea.
3. **Sentence 1:** the container replaced all this, cutting time in port from days to hours.
4. **Sentence 4:** the cost of shipping fell so far it no longer shaped decisions about where to make things.

**Why 2 is odd:** It is on the same topic, containers and costs, but it *contradicts* sentence 1. Sentence 1 says time in port fell "from days to hours"; sentence 2 says "loading times barely changed". It also leaves 5's problem (long port times) without the solution that 1 supplies.

You eliminate it by finding the contradiction, not by spotting a change of topic.

## Passage 2: Recommendation and taste (technology and society; moderate)

**How the passage is built:**
1. **The debate is about diversity.** Critics say recommendations narrow what people consume; defenders say they widen it.
2. **The author's turn:** taste is a *capacity* to discriminate, not an inventory. It is formed by choosing, being disappointed and reflecting.
3. **Recommendation systems remove disappointment,** so consumption could be broad while judgement weakens.
4. **The objection that taste was always guided.** Critics gave reasons that could be disputed; algorithms give correlations with nothing to argue against.
5. **The designers ask the wrong question:** variety, when they should be asking whether users become better judges.

### Q8. Answer: D (author vs both sides; moderate)

**Evidence:** "Both sides measure the same thing—the variety of what people consume—and it is not obvious that this is the right measure."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** The defenders *cite* the injection of novelty.
- **(B)** Applies to the critics only, and the author doesn't judge whether they exaggerate.
- **(C)** Neither side is said to assume that algorithms give reasons.

### Q9. Answer: D (phrase in context; moderate)

**Evidence:** "Taste is not an inventory… What we mean by taste is closer to a capacity."

**Why D is right:** The contrast in the passage is between a stock or collection and an ability.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Nothing is said about taste being unchangeable.
- **(B)** This is the closest trap. It sounds algorithmic, but the passage contrasts collecting with capacity, not storable records with something unpredictable.
- **(C)** Describes what critics do, not what taste is mistaken for.

### Q10. Answer: B (application to a hypothetical change; moderate)

**The "right question":** whether users "are becoming better judges". The author says reasons that can be disputed help with this: "one can read a review, disagree with it and… discover what one values."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Variety is explicitly "the wrong question".
- **(C)** Learning tastes faster serves user satisfaction, the very thing that removes disappointment.
- **(D)** This is the strongest rival. Critics' approval is still guidance *without reasons given to the user*. It leaves "nothing to argue with".

### Q11. Answer: C (EXCEPT; easy)

**Why C is the exception:** The passage says the old intermediaries had "snobberies". It never says algorithms are distorted by prejudice, and it never says critics were free of it.

**What the passage does suggest:**
- **(A)** They "steered people towards some works and away from others".
- **(B)** "Reasons can be disputed".
- **(D)** They "at least attempted to address" whether people became better judges.

## Verbal Ability, Questions 12–13

### Q12. Answer: A (summary; moderate)

**Why A is right:** It keeps the structure-versus-practice contrast, which the skeleton metaphor makes vivid.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B)** This is the closest trap. It accepts the linguists' consolation and leaves out the paragraph's point that what made the language alive is lost.
- **(C)** Contradicted: a grammar "can tell a future reader how sentences were formed".
- **(D)** The claim about what speakers believe is invented.

### Q13. Answer: C, gap 3 (sentence placement; moderate)

**What the sentence needs:** "The distinction matters because the public tends to hear *such a probability* as if it were *a date*." It needs the probabilistic forecast example ("a one-in-three chance… within thirty years") immediately before it. The "date" picks up the prediction ("next Tuesday").

| Gap | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1 | Locally plausible, since "the distinction" picks up "distinguish". But "such a probability" has no antecedent yet. |
| 2 | Follows the *prediction* example, which gives a date, not a probability. |
| **3** | **Correct.** It follows the forecast's probability directly. |
| 4 | "Such a probability" is now two sentences back, and the insertion would separate "Forecasts, *by contrast*" from the sentence about predictions that it contrasts with. |

*Audit note:* the first draft ("hear the second as if it were the first") fitted equally well at gap 1. The sentence was rewritten to tie it to the forecast example.

## Passage 3: Book review on public health and theory (moderate)

**How the passage is built:**
1. **The triumphalist story:** germ theory replaced miasma, and mortality fell.
2. **The book's chronology:** mortality fell first, through sanitation carried out by believers in miasma. A wrong theory produced the right interventions.
3. **The reviewer's objection:** the book over-generalises that theory matters little. Wrong theories work only where their advice happens to match the truth. Flushing sewage into rivers shows what happens where the two diverge.
4. **Practice can outrun understanding "only on credit".** Germ theory explained which measures to keep.
5. **Verdict:** a brilliant book that replaces one simple story with its mirror image.

### Q14. Answer: B (reviewer's assessment; easy)

**Evidence:** "much of its demolition is persuasive… The argument proves less than it claims… replace one simple story with its mirror image."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** The reviewer accepts the chronology.
- **(C)** This is the book's moral, which the reviewer rejects.
- **(D)** The reviewer never calls miasma theory "largely correct". The theory was wrong, and its measures worked anyway.

### Q15. Answer: A (purpose of an example; moderate)

**Evidence:** "Where miasma and germs pointed in the same direction, the reformers prospered; where they diverged, the theory killed people."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B)** The reformers' motives are never questioned.
- **(C)** The passage doesn't explain why germ theory came to be accepted.
- **(D)** The chronology isn't doubted.

### Q16. Answer: D (figurative phrase; moderate)

**Evidence:** "A practice grounded in a false theory succeeds by luck of alignment, and it has no way of knowing when its luck will run out."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Unsupported. Practice doesn't generate understanding by itself; germ theory had to supply it.
- **(B)** Contradicted: the sanitarians' practice succeeded without a correct theory.
- **(C)** A literal-minded misreading of "credit".

### Q17. Answer: C (application to a new case; difficult)

**What the fourth paragraph describes:** a practice that works by luck of alignment and fails where its theory diverges from the truth, until a correct explanation sorts its successes from its failures.

**Why C is right:** A remedy that helps with one illness but harms with a similar one, until its working is explained, has the same structure.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** This is the strongest rival. An unexplained treatment that has never failed is *compatible* with the argument, but it doesn't display the argument's key feature: luck running out where theory and truth diverge.
- **(B)** A correct theory that recommends no change in practice doesn't bear on the argument.
- **(D)** Neglect of a correct theory is a different phenomenon.

## Verbal Ability, Question 18

### Q18. Answer: 3 (odd sentence out; moderate)

**The coherent paragraph is 4-1-5-2:**
1. **Sentence 4:** honesty stalls depend on trust that is hard to explain.
2. **Sentence 1:** no one would ever know if you didn't pay.
3. **Sentence 5:** "Yet" most people pay.
4. **Sentence 2:** they pay to stay, in their own eyes, the kind of person who pays, not to avoid detection.

**Why 3 is odd:** It shares the topic, but it contradicts two premises. Sentence 1 says "no one would ever know"; sentence 3 says non-payers are "usually caught by cameras". Sentence 3 also undercuts 2's explanation that detection is beside the point.

## Passage 4: Rewilding and baselines (ecology; easiest)

**How the passage is built:**
1. **Which past?** The choice of baseline determines what counts as success.
2. **Shifting baselines:** each generation's idea of what is normal drifts down.
3. **Pre-human baselines** often can't be reconstructed or sustained, which gives "a garden… labelled as wilderness".
4. **The process approach:** restore processes such as grazing and flooding, not lists of species. The grazing matters more than the grazer.
5. **The weakness:** the approach risks being successful by definition. The compromise is to use indicators as warning lights.

### Q19. Answer: D (purpose of an example; easy)

**Evidence:** "the standard of what is natural drifts downward without anyone noticing."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Reverses the example. The fisherman *underestimates* past abundance.
- **(B)** Wrong paragraph and wrong claim.
- **(C)** No cause of decline is given.

### Q20. Answer: A (phrase in context; easy)

**Evidence:** "may require continual intervention to maintain—a garden, in effect, labelled as wilderness."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B)** This is the closest trap. Introducing substitute animals is discussed in paragraph 4, as part of the *process* approach, and with approval. The garden phrase is about the need for continual intervention.
- **(C)** Visitor access is never mentioned.
- **(D)** Nothing is said about a project failing to attract species.

### Q21. Answer: C (author's attitude; easy)

**Evidence:** "A more promising approach…", "The approach has its own weakness…", "The answer is probably a compromise."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Overreaches. The author qualifies the approach rather than dismissing it.
- **(B)** Contradicted: the landscape "may resemble no particular past at all".
- **(D)** Reverses the passage: the approach is "harder to evaluate".

### Q22. Answer: D (EXCEPT; easy)

**Why D is the exception:** Opposition from local residents is never mentioned.

**The difficulties the passage does give:**
- **(A)** "No such state within reach of reconstruction".
- **(B)** "Climate, soils… changed so much that the old community… could not persist".
- **(C)** "May require continual intervention".

## Verbal Ability, Questions 23–24

### Q23. Answer: B (summary; moderate)

**Why B is right:** It keeps the concession (tests are crude) and the counterpoint (tests are open to outsiders, unlike the interviews they replaced).

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Contradicted: a single number is "a crude measure". The paragraph doesn't claim greater accuracy.
- **(C)** This is the closest trap. The paragraph says "The criticism is fair". It adds context; it does not reject the criticism.
- **(D)** The paragraph makes no recommendation.

### Q24. Answer: D, gap 4 (sentence placement; moderate)

**What the sentence needs:** "The problem, *it turned out*, was not the wait but the boredom of it." "It turned out" reports a discovery, so it must come after the experiment that revealed it.

| Gap | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1 | Too early. Nothing has yet been tried. |
| 2 | Locally plausible, because it follows the engineers' failure. But the discovery would then come before the mirrors that revealed it. |
| 3 | The result hasn't been reported yet. |
| **4** | **Correct.** The complaints fell although the wait was unchanged, which leads straight to the conclusion "it turned out". |

## Diagnostic guide

| If you missed… | Work on this |
|---|---|
| Q3, Q6 | Locating the author's own principle, then attacking its premise rather than its application |
| Q4 | Checking both halves of each pair in pairing questions |
| Q9, Q16 | Reading figurative phrases through the sentence around them |
| Q10, Q17 | Matching a new case to the *structure* of the argument, not just to its topic |
| Q7, Q18 | Odd sentence out by contradiction |
| Q2, Q13, Q24 | Time words in sentence placement ("even then", "it turned out") and contrast words that must stay adjacent ("by contrast", "yet") |

**Exam-day takeaway:** in a real CAT, your score depends as much on which questions you choose to attempt as on accuracy. In this paper the easiest marks are Passage 4, Q1, Q11 and Q14. The hardest are concentrated in Passage 1.
