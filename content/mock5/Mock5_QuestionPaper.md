## Passage 1 (Questions 3–6)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

Why should a court today be bound by what another court decided fifty years ago? The question is more awkward than it looks. If the earlier decision was right, the present court would presumably reach the same result on its own; if it was wrong, following it merely repeats the error. On either branch, precedent seems to add nothing except the possibility of perpetuating mistakes, and its authority appears to rest on little more than the accident of which case happened to arrive first.

The standard defences point to values that correctness alone cannot secure. People arrange their affairs—sign contracts, buy property, plan businesses—in reliance on the law as it has been declared, and a rule that may be overturned at any time is a poor foundation for such plans. Treating like cases alike is also a requirement of fairness: two litigants with indistinguishable claims should not receive different answers merely because they appeared before different judges or in different decades. And a system in which every question could be reopened in every case would be ruinously slow. Predictability also constrains judges themselves, making it harder for a court to decide a case according to the sympathies of the moment.

These defences are sound, but they do not apply with equal force everywhere. Reliance matters most where people actually rely: on the rules governing commercial transactions or the transfer of land, where the content of the rule often matters less than its being settled and known. It matters least where no one could reasonably have planned around the rule, and where the cost of an error falls on people who had no say in it. A precedent that misreads the scope of a basic liberty is not made more tolerable by the fact that institutions have grown accustomed to it. The strength of precedent should therefore vary with the kind of question it settles—strongest where settledness is the main good, weakest where it is the error itself that endures.

Courts rarely say anything so explicit. They have instead developed the technique of distinguishing: a precedent is not overruled but confined to its facts, the present case being declared different in some respect that makes the earlier reasoning inapplicable. Distinguishing allows the law to change while preserving the appearance of continuity, and in moderation it is a reasonable way of testing an old rule against circumstances its authors did not foresee. Used without restraint, it produces a different problem. Each distinction adds an exception, each exception invites further argument about its boundaries, and a body of law that was once simple becomes a thicket that only specialists can navigate.

The irony is that a technique meant to protect the value of predictability can end by destroying it. A rule riddled with distinctions may be formally intact and practically unknowable—binding in name, but no longer able to tell anyone in advance what they may do.

## Passage 2 (Questions 8–11)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

Arguments about recommendation algorithms usually turn on diversity. Critics charge that systems which suggest what to watch, read or listen to next confine users to ever-narrower versions of what they already like, so that taste contracts into a feedback loop. Defenders reply that the evidence is mixed at best: many users encounter a wider range of material than they did when choice was limited to what a local shop happened to stock, and some systems deliberately inject novelty to keep users from growing bored. Each side can cite studies, and the studies are hard to compare, since they define diversity in different ways.

Both sides measure the same thing—the variety of what people consume—and it is not obvious that this is the right measure. Taste is not an inventory. A person with a vast collection of recordings may have very little taste, and a person who has heard only a few may have a great deal. What we mean by taste is closer to a capacity: the ability to discriminate, to say why one thing is better than another, to recognise quality in an unfamiliar form. And that capacity appears to be formed less by exposure than by a particular kind of experience—choosing for oneself, being disappointed, and working out what went wrong.

Recommendation systems are designed to reduce exactly that experience. Their measure of success is that the user is satisfied with the next item and stays for the one after. A system that worked perfectly would never disappoint, and in never disappointing it would deprive the user of the occasions on which judgement is exercised and refined. Disappointment, on this account, is not a defect in the experience of choosing but its most instructive part. The result need not be narrower consumption. It could be broad consumption by people progressively less able to say what they think of any of it.

It might be objected that taste has always been guided. Critics, booksellers and knowledgeable friends steered people towards some works and away from others long before any algorithm did. The difference lies less in the fact of guidance than in its form. A critic gives reasons, and reasons can be disputed; one can read a review, disagree with it and, in the course of disagreeing, discover what one values. A recommendation gives no reasons beyond the bare fact of a correlation—people who liked this also liked that—and offers nothing to argue with.

None of this requires that the systems be abandoned. It suggests rather that their designers have been answering the wrong question. Whether users see enough variety is a question about what passes before them. Whether they are becoming better judges of it is a question about what happens in them, and it is the second that the old intermediaries, for all their snobberies, at least attempted to address. That question is harder to measure, which may be why it is so rarely asked.

## Passage 3 (Questions 14–17)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

The history of public health has long been told as a story of knowledge triumphing over superstition. Physicians in the mid-nineteenth century, so the story runs, believed that disease arose from miasma—foul air rising from rotting matter—until the germ theory replaced their error with truth, and mortality fell as a result. The new history under review sets out to dismantle this account, and much of its demolition is persuasive.

Its central exhibit is chronology. In many cities, deaths from waterborne disease began to decline well before germ theory won acceptance among physicians, let alone officials. The decline followed the construction of sewers, the paving of streets and the provision of piped water—works undertaken largely by reformers who believed in miasma. Cities that built sewers early saw cholera and typhoid recede, while cities that delayed continued to suffer, whatever their physicians believed. The author's point is that a mistaken theory produced the right interventions: if bad smells cause disease, then removing filth removes the smells, and removing filth also, as it happens, removes the bacteria. The reformers were wrong about why their measures worked, and the measures worked anyway.

So far, so instructive. The difficulty arises when the author moves from this case to a general moral, suggesting that the history of medicine shows theory to matter much less than practice, and that the achievements usually credited to scientific understanding were more often the fruit of practical trial and error. The argument proves less than it claims. A wrong theory produces right interventions only when its recommendations happen to coincide with those a true theory would make, and the book's own evidence shows how fragile that coincidence was. The same conviction that smells were the danger led some authorities to flush sewage into rivers, removing the stench from the streets while delivering the contamination to the drinking water downstream. Where miasma and germs pointed in the same direction, the reformers prospered; where they diverged, the theory killed people.

What the episode actually suggests is that practice can outrun understanding for a while, but only on credit. A practice grounded in a false theory succeeds by luck of alignment, and it has no way of knowing when its luck will run out. A sanitarian who did not know why clean water mattered could not know which of his measures to keep when money ran short. Germ theory did not merely ratify what the sanitarians had already achieved; it explained why their successes were successes and their failures failures, and so made it possible to keep the one and stop the other.

This is a substantial and often brilliant book, and its revision of the triumphalist narrative will stand. Its account of the sanitary movement, in particular, deserves to become the standard one. It would have been more brilliant still had its author resisted the temptation to replace one simple story with its mirror image.

## Passage 4 (Questions 19–22)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

Projects that aim to return land to a wilder state face a question that sounds simple and is not: wild as of when? A wetland drained two centuries ago, a forest cleared before records began, a grassland shaped for millennia by grazing animals that have since disappeared—each could be restored to a different past, and each past would produce a different landscape. The choice of a baseline determines what counts as success, and there is no obviously correct one to choose.

Part of the difficulty is psychological. Ecologists have described a tendency for each generation to treat the condition of nature in its own youth as the normal state of affairs, against which later losses are measured. A fisherman who remembers the catches of his first years at sea regards them as the baseline, unaware that his predecessors would have considered them evidence of a depleted sea. Over several generations, the standard of what is natural drifts downward without anyone noticing. The same drift has been documented for birds, wildflowers and the size of trees.

One response is to reach back as far as possible, to a state before people altered the land at all. But in many regions there is no such state within reach of reconstruction, and even where there is, the climate, soils and surrounding landscape have changed so much that the old community of species could not persist. Even the attempt to reconstruct such a state depends on fragmentary evidence—pollen, bones, old accounts—that can support several pictures at once. Restoring a list of species that lived somewhere ten thousand years ago may require continual intervention to maintain—a garden, in effect, labelled as wilderness.

A more promising approach shifts attention from composition to process. Instead of specifying which species should be present at the end, it asks which processes should be allowed to operate: grazing by large herbivores, the flooding of rivers, the spread of natural disturbance, the movement of predators. Where the original grazers are extinct, hardy cattle or horses may be brought in to do their work, since it is the grazing, not the grazer, that matters. Restore those, the argument runs, and the landscape will find its own form, which may resemble no particular past at all. The goal is not a picture but a set of dynamics.

The approach has its own weakness. A goal defined by processes is harder to evaluate than one defined by species, because almost any outcome can be described as the result of processes having been allowed to operate. Without some account of which outcomes would count as failure, a process-based project risks becoming successful by definition, whatever happens. The answer is probably a compromise: processes as the goal, with a small number of indicators—the return of certain species, the recovery of soil or water quality—treated not as targets but as warning lights.
