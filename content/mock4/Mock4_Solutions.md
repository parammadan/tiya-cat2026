## Passage 1: What entitles us to believe testimony? (epistemology; difficult)

**Structure of the passage:**
- **Reductionism:** you need independent reasons to trust a speaker. *Problem:* those reasons themselves rest on testimony.
- **The default view:** trust unless you have a reason to doubt. *Problem:* it makes the credulous hearer as justified as the careful one.
- **The author's view:** entitlement is a *disposition*, meaning you are responsive to signs of unreliability. Trust is "monitored as it goes".
- **Children** illustrate this: generous trust, but not blind trust. Trust is "held on condition".

### Q1. Answer: B — author's own view (difficult)

**Evidence:** The careful listener "is responsive to signs that something is amiss… and would withdraw her trust if they appeared. Her acceptance is not earned in advance."

**Why B is right:** B pairs a disposition to respond with no prior verification. Those are the two features the author names.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** This is reductionism.
- **(C)** The strongest rival. It describes the default view: having no reason for suspicion at the moment you accept. The author's view turns on whether you *would* respond if signs appeared, not on whether you happen to have none. The credulous hearer meets C's condition. That is exactly the author's objection.
- **(D)** This turns some of the *signs* the passage lists into sufficient conditions. On the author's view the signs are things to stay alert to, not a checklist that grants entitlement.

**Takeaway:** "Has no reason to doubt" (a state) and "would notice reasons to doubt" (a disposition) can look identical in an option. Telling them apart is the whole question.

### Q2. Answer: D — weaken the author's objection (very difficult)

**The objection:** On the default view, "the credulous hearer, who never looks for such grounds, is as well justified as the careful one."

**Why D is right:** The objection depends on the credulous hearer *meeting* the default condition (no reason to doubt) without ever having looked. Suppose only hearers who have searched for reasons to doubt can meet that condition. Then the credulous hearer fails it, the default view does separate careful hearers from credulous ones, and the objection loses its force.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** That speakers are mostly sincere might make default trust *pay off*. It doesn't stop the default view ranking credulous and careful hearers equally.
- **(B)** This supports the motive behind the objection: careful hearing matters.
- **(C)** This concerns the reductionist's problem with children, not the default view.

**Takeaway:** To weaken an objection, attack the premise it needs. Here that premise is that the credulous hearer qualifies.

### Q3. Answer: A — purpose of an example (moderate)

**Evidence:** "The inductive base from which testimony is supposed to be justified cannot be assembled without already relying on testimony."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B)** The passage doesn't question how reliable encyclopaedias are.
- **(C)** This misattributes the example. The encyclopaedia example targets reductionism. The "too easy" complaint is aimed at the default view, separately.
- **(D)** This reverses the point: checking against other sources is itself testimony-dependent.

### Q4. Answer: D — purpose of evidence (difficult)

**Evidence:** Children "accept a great deal… since they have no general record of adults' reliability to draw on". "But they do not accept it indiscriminately… they become wary." "Their trust is generous, but it is not blind."

**Why D is right:** Generous-but-conditional trust is exactly the disposition view.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Overstates. "Proves" is too strong, and the evidence is offered *against* the default view as much as for it ("not blind").
- **(B)** The strongest rival. Children do prefer informants who have been accurate, a local and recent record. But the passage stresses that they trust widely *without* any general record of adults' reliability. B leaves out the generous half of the evidence and so recasts the children as reductionists.
- **(C)** The passage never says trust is learned through being misled.

## Passage 2: Why we admire unfinished works (aesthetics; moderate–difficult)

**Structure of the passage:**
1. **Documentary explanation.** *Problem:* it is second-hand. A notebook or a film would serve as well, and a sketch would move us as much.
2. **Invitation explanation.** It is closer to the experience, but needs a qualification: the existing parts must suggest how the missing ones continue.
3. **Historical complication.** Modern art taught us to value incompleteness, and we now apply that value retrospectively.
4. **Conclusion.** The admiration is not mistaken. "Unfinished" is an intersection of object, viewer and history.

### Q5. Answer: C — author's objection (moderate)

**Evidence:** "a well-preserved notebook or a film of the artist at work would serve as well or better".

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** The author never says the documentary view exaggerates what the work reveals.
- **(B)** Deliberate incompleteness comes up only in the historical paragraph, as a point about *modern* art. It isn't the objection to this view.
- **(D)** This is an invented confusion between process and intended form.

### Q6. Answer: A — purpose of an example (moderate)

**Evidence:** "What invites is a particular kind of incompleteness—one in which the parts that exist establish enough of a direction…"

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B)** Reverses the point: the half-missing canvas *fails* to invite.
- **(C)** Overreach. The author says the invitation view is "closer to what the encounter actually feels like". It is qualified, not rejected.
- **(D)** Unsupported. The point is that mere lack differs from a suggestive gap.

### Q7. Answer: B — inference with cross-paragraph check (difficult)

**Evidence:** "Our readiness… owes a good deal to the art of the last century and a half… The admiration is genuine, but it is in part retrospective."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(D)** The strongest rival. "Retrospective" can sound like "anachronistic error". But the very next paragraph begins "None of this makes the admiration mistaken." You need both paragraphs to reject D.
- **(A)** Contradicted: the sculptor "may have regarded [it] only as a failure".
- **(C)** Reverses the passage. Modern art made incompleteness a deliberate choice.

### Q8. Answer: A — implication of the conclusion (difficult)

**Evidence:** "'unfinished' names… an intersection—of a particular structure in the object, a habit of attention in the viewer and a history that taught that habit." "To ask whether an unfinished work succeeds is therefore to ask about all three at once."

**Why A is right:** If the answer depends on three factors, examining the object alone can't settle it.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B)** Overreach. The author redescribes the category but doesn't drop it.
- **(C)** Contradicted: "there is no reason why an effect that arose by accident should be less real".
- **(D)** An unsupported ranking. The three factors are presented together; none is said to matter more.

## Passage 3: What metaphors mean (philosophy of language; difficult)

**Structure of the passage:**
1. **Ornament view.** Refuted: paraphrases always fail.
2. **Special-meaning view.** It doesn't follow from that failure, as the joke and melody analogies show.
3. **Economical view.** Literal meaning plus provoked attention. Right about *living* metaphors.
4. **But dead metaphors leave new literal senses behind,** which the economical view can't explain.
5. **Reconciliation.** Living metaphors have no special meaning but have a "career". Accepted ways of seeing harden into senses.

### Q9. Answer: D — purpose of an analogy (moderate)

**Evidence:** "That something cannot be paraphrased shows only that it is not a statement awaiting translation… A joke cannot be paraphrased either, and we do not infer that jokes have a secret 'humorous meaning'."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** The strongest rival. Jokes and metaphors may well lose their force when explained, but that isn't what the analogy is used to *argue*. Its job is to block an inference: "cannot be paraphrased, therefore it has a special meaning".
- **(B)** Reverses the analogy's point.
- **(C)** Absurd overextension.

### Q10. Answer: C — author vs cited view (difficult)

**Evidence:** "This view is right about living metaphors and wrong about what becomes of them… it would be a mystery how they could leave new literal meanings behind."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Reverses the view. The economical view is the one that *emphasises* open-endedness.
- **(B)** The author *agrees* with this part ("right about living metaphors").
- **(D)** This is the ornament view from paragraph 1, not the economical view.

### Q11. Answer: B — application (easy)

**Why B is right:** "The foot of a mountain", used "with no thought of a body", parallels "the leg of a table… without provoking anyone to notice anything".

**Why the others are wrong:** A, C and D are each *living* uses, chosen to provoke a way of seeing: a poem, a joke and a heuristic.

### Q12. Answer: A — synthesis of the conclusion (difficult)

**Evidence:** "A living metaphor has no special meaning, but it has a career… when enough speakers accept the invitation in the same way, the way of seeing becomes a sense." The wording of A differs from the passage: "means just what its words say" for "no special meaning", and "shared ways of seeing can become senses" for "when enough speakers accept the invitation in the same way, the way of seeing becomes a sense".

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B)** The strongest rival. It sounds like the "career" story, but it gives living metaphors a special meaning, which the author denies.
- **(C)** Contradicted: "not a second kind of meaning".
- **(D)** Misreads the passage. Living metaphors *do* carry meaning: their ordinary literal meaning.

## Passage 4: Bureaucracy and impersonality (social theory; moderate)

**Structure of the passage:**
1. **The complaint:** bureaucracy is cold. **The reply:** impersonality guarantees equal treatment.
2. **Both are wrong** to treat impersonality as a property of rules. It is a fragile achievement, "the discipline of judgement".
3. **In practice,** officials adapt the rules invisibly.
4. **So the question** is where discretion sits and whether it can be seen.
5. **Conclusion:** strict impersonality can reduce equality by driving judgement out of sight.

### Q13. Answer: C — author vs both cited views (moderate)

**Evidence:** "Both the complaint and the reply assume that impersonality is something an office either has or lacks, as though it were a property of its rules. It is better understood as an achievement."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** The author never disputes that impersonality protects against favouritism.
- **(B)** How applicants experience bureaucracy is not the point of disagreement.
- **(D)** The author says interpretation is unavoidable. Whether it should be *permitted* never arises.

### Q14. Answer: D — strengthen (difficult)

**The conclusion:** demanding strict impersonality "may produce less of the equality it was meant to secure". Equality is "better served by judgement that is visible".

**Why D is right:** If similar applicants are treated more alike where reasons are recorded than where departures are banned, that is direct evidence for the conclusion.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Officials' stated motives don't bear on whether banning discretion reduces equality.
- **(B)** Applicants' *perceptions* of fairness aren't measures of equal treatment. If anything, B points the other way.
- **(C)** This weakens a premise: if interpretation is rarely difficult, there is little hidden discretion to worry about.

### Q15. Answer: B — inference (moderate)

**Evidence:** "the official record shows only that the rule was applied. The office looks impersonal on paper."

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Extreme: the passage says nothing about deliberate falsification.
- **(C)** Contradicted. The adaptations are "invisible" in the record, so the studies must rely on other sources.
- **(D)** Misreads the passage. The record *shows* mechanical application; it doesn't prove that application happened.

### Q16. Answer: C — phrase in context (easy)

**Evidence:** "not the absence of judgement but the discipline of judgement—the official's effort to set aside what she happens to feel".

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Plays on the punitive sense of "discipline".
- **(B)** Contradicted: rules must always be interpreted.
- **(D)** Takes "discipline" in the sense of a field of study. The passage never says this.

## Verbal Ability

### Q17. Answer: 4312 — para jumble (moderate)

**Order:**
1. **Sentence 4** makes the claim: the demand for originality is recent.
2. **Sentence 3** supports it: for centuries, artists were praised for handling inherited subjects.
3. **Sentence 1** follows with "Imitating… was, *accordingly*, the ordinary path". "Accordingly" depends on 3's norm.
4. **Sentence 2** closes with "Only when… did novelty become a virtue". This explains how the modern demand arose.

**Alternative orders rejected:**
- **4-1-3-2:** "Accordingly" right after 4 would draw imitation from the bare claim about dates. It has nothing to follow from.
- **4-3-2-1:** This ends on the old norm after the modern turn has already been explained. That breaks the movement from past to present.

### Q18. Answer: 2431 — para jumble (moderate)

**Order:**
1. **Sentence 2** introduces the authority of numbers.
2. **Sentence 4** explains why: a figure "seems to speak for itself".
3. **Sentence 3** follows with "*That appearance*", which refers to 4's "seems". It is most valuable where trust is weak.
4. **Sentence 1** concludes with "*on this view*": quantification is a substitute for confidence.

**Locked links:**
- 3 needs 4 for "That appearance" to refer back to.
- 1 needs 3's point about trust for "on this view".

### Q19. Answer: 2 — odd sentence out (difficult)

**The coherent paragraph is 4-5-1-3:**
1. **Sentence 4:** forgetting is treated as a failure.
2. **Sentence 5:** "Yet" a total memory would be useless.
3. **Sentence 1:** generalising requires letting go of particulars.
4. **Sentence 3:** "on this view" forgetting is an instrument of learning.

**Why 2 is odd:** It shares the topic and the key word. But it *contradicts* the paragraph's central step. Sentence 1 says generalising requires letting go of particulars, so forgetting serves learning by discarding particulars and keeping the pattern. Sentence 2 says the opposite happens: we lose the pattern first and keep vivid particulars.

It can't be inserted anywhere. Placed after 4, it would be offered in support of the failure view, but then 5's "Yet… nearly useless" and 1's mechanism directly contradict it.

*Audit note:* the first draft's odd sentence (the shape of the forgetting curve) could be eliminated just by noticing a change of aspect, so it was replaced with one that you have to argue away.

### Q20. Answer: 3 — odd sentence out (difficult)

**The coherent paragraph is 4-1-5-2:**
1. **Sentence 4:** a rest is part of the music.
2. **Sentence 1:** "Its length" is specified exactly.
3. **Sentence 5:** what the listener hears in a rest depends on context.
4. **Sentence 2:** composers have "exploited this".

**Why 3 is odd:** It shares the topic and the vocabulary (rest, length, performers), but it *contradicts* the paragraph. Sentence 1 says changing a rest's length "changes the piece", while 3 says its length is "a matter of taste". Sentence 3 also contradicts 4, which says a rest is "not an absence of music".

**Takeaway:** When every sentence is on topic, look for the one that conflicts with a premise.

### Q21. Answer: A — para summary (difficult)

**Why A is right:** It keeps both halves of the paragraph. Lasting differences are real (against the overreaction), and they are circumstantial, not essences (against the old talk).

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B)** The strongest trap. "Vindicate the older idea" ignores the author's point that the old idea's error *was* treating differences as essences. Only the observation of differences is vindicated, not the idea.
- **(C)** This is the overreaction the author criticises.
- **(D)** Contradicted: the differences "outlast migration by a generation or more".

### Q22. Answer: D — para summary (difficult)

**Why D is right:** It keeps the condition (errors scattered around the truth require partial knowledge) and the conclusion (crowds pool expertise, they don't replace it).

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** Keeps "more accurate than experts" as a general claim and adds a single exception. It misses the main point: the crowd *depends on* distributed knowledge.
- **(B)** Keeps the inference "expertise is overrated", which the author rejects.
- **(C)** A prescription the paragraph never makes.

### Q23. Answer: B, gap 2 — sentence placement (moderate)

**The sentence:** "Print changed *this* by making every copy… identical." "This" must refer to the problem of mismatched pagination and chapter-or-verse references.

| Gap | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1 | Locally plausible, because the problem has just been stated. But the next sentence ("A reference… had to name a chapter") is still about the world before print, so placing the sentence here breaks the chronology. |
| **2** | **Correct.** The problem is complete, and the next sentence ("For the first time…") states the consequence of identical copies. |
| 3 | Comes after the consequence. It would explain the cause after the effect, and "this" would have no clear referent. |
| 4 | Too late. |

### Q24. Answer: C, gap 3 — sentence placement (moderate)

**The sentence:** "Introduce a mismatch… and the dampening *should* weaken." This is a *prediction*, so it belongs between the theory and the experiment that tests it.

| Gap | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1 | No forecast has been described yet. |
| 2 | Locally plausible, because the dampening has just been mentioned. But it would break the link between "dampened" and the next sentence, which begins "A self-produced touch is *therefore*…". |
| **3** | **Correct.** Theory, then prediction ("should"), then the delay experiment that confirms it. |
| 4 | It would state the prediction after the result. "Should" no longer fits there. |

## Diagnostic guide

| If you missed… | Work on this |
|---|---|
| Q1, Q10, Q13 | In "view, view, my view" passages, match options to the owner of each view |
| Q2, Q14 | Finding the premise an objection or conclusion depends on |
| Q4, Q7 | Checking a tempting option against the *next* paragraph, which often qualifies it |
| Q9, Q12 | Telling the *job* an analogy does apart from what it happens to say |
| Q19, Q20 | Odd-one-out by level of claim or by contradiction, not by topic |
| Q23, Q24 | Using causal and chronological order ("therefore", "should") in sentence placement |
