## Passage 1 (Questions 1–4)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

Most of what any of us knows, we know because someone told us. We have not measured the distance to the sun, witnessed the battles of the last century or tested the drugs we take; we have read, or heard, or been taught. The philosophical question is what entitles us to believe what we are told, and two answers have dominated the discussion.

The first, which may be called reductionist, holds that testimony is never a source of justification in its own right. We are entitled to accept what a speaker says only when we have independent reasons—a record of past reliability, say, or knowledge of the speaker's expertise—for thinking that speakers of that kind, on topics of that kind, tend to be right. The second view denies this. Testimony, it holds, is a basic source of knowledge, like perception or memory: we are entitled to accept what we are told by default, unless we have some positive reason to doubt it.

Each view has an embarrassment it cannot easily escape. The reductionist's independent reasons turn out, on inspection, to be shot through with testimony themselves. I believe that encyclopaedias are generally reliable because I have been told so, or because I have checked some of their claims against other sources that I accepted on someone's word. The inductive base from which testimony is supposed to be justified cannot be assembled without already relying on testimony. The default view, by contrast, makes justification too cheap. If I am entitled to believe whatever I am told unless I happen to have grounds for suspicion, then the credulous hearer, who never looks for such grounds, is as well justified as the careful one.

A more promising thought locates entitlement neither in the reasons the hearer possesses nor in a default the hearer enjoys, but in a disposition the hearer has. What distinguishes the careful listener from the credulous one is not that she has checked the speaker's credentials before every acceptance, which no one could do, but that she is responsive to signs that something is amiss—hesitation, implausibility, a conflict of interest, a claim that sits badly with what else she knows—and would withdraw her trust if they appeared. Her acceptance is not earned in advance; it is monitored as it goes.

Studies of young children lend this picture some support. Children accept a great deal of what adults tell them, which the reductionist would find hard to justify, since they have no general record of adults' reliability to draw on. But they do not accept it indiscriminately. Quite early, they prefer informants who have been accurate to informants who have erred, and they become wary of those who have something to gain from their belief. Their trust is generous, but it is not blind. If that is what testimonial entitlement looks like at its origin, it is neither earned nor free; it is held on condition.

## Passage 2 (Questions 5–8)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

Museums are full of works their makers did not finish: canvases abandoned with a corner bare, statues whose figures seem to struggle out of the block, novels that stop mid-chapter. Many of them are among the most admired objects in their collections, and the admiration needs explaining. A finished work, after all, is what its maker intended; an unfinished one is, on the face of it, a work that failed to become what it was meant to be.

The usual explanation is documentary. The unfinished work, we are told, lets us see the artist at work—the underdrawing beneath the paint, the chisel marks that the polished surface would have erased—and so admits us to a process that completion conceals. There is truth in this, but it makes the appeal of the unfinished oddly second-hand. If what we value is evidence of process, then a well-preserved notebook or a film of the artist at work would serve as well or better, and the unfinished object would be merely a convenient source of information about something else. It would also leave unexplained why a sketch, which reveals process just as plainly, so often moves us less than an abandoned painting.

A second explanation locates the value in the viewer rather than the maker. Incompleteness, on this view, is an invitation: the bare corner and the half-emerged figure enlist the imagination in supplying what is missing, and the work is experienced not as a fixed object but as a field of possibilities. This is closer to what the encounter actually feels like, but it proves too much if left unqualified. A canvas with its lower half missing does not invite completion; it simply lacks something. What invites is a particular kind of incompleteness—one in which the parts that exist establish enough of a direction that the parts that do not can be imagined as continuations rather than guessed at as blanks.

There is also a historical complication. Our readiness to find unfinished works compelling owes a good deal to the art of the last century and a half, in which fragments, sketches and visible process became deliberate choices rather than accidents. Having learned to value incompleteness where it was intended, we have extended the same regard to works in which it was not. The admiration is genuine, but it is in part retrospective: we see in an abandoned Renaissance block an effect that its sculptor may have regarded only as a failure.

None of this makes the admiration mistaken. A work's meaning has never been confined to what its maker intended, and there is no reason why an effect that arose by accident should be less real than one that was planned. But it does suggest that "unfinished" names not a single aesthetic category but an intersection—of a particular structure in the object, a habit of attention in the viewer and a history that taught that habit. To ask whether an unfinished work succeeds is therefore to ask about all three at once.

## Passage 3 (Questions 9–12)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

For a long time the standard account of metaphor treated it as a decoration. To call a man a lion was to say, in a heightened way, that he was brave, and the figure could in principle be replaced by its literal equivalent without loss of content, though with some loss of colour. The account fell out of favour for a good reason: the replacements never work. Any attempt to spell out what a rich metaphor means produces either a list that goes on indefinitely or a flat statement that misses what made the original worth saying.

The usual conclusion drawn from this failure is that metaphors carry a special kind of meaning—a metaphorical meaning, over and above the literal one, which resists paraphrase because it is not propositional in the ordinary way. This conclusion does not follow. That something cannot be paraphrased shows only that it is not a statement awaiting translation; it does not show that it is a statement of a stranger kind. A joke cannot be paraphrased either, and we do not infer that jokes have a secret "humorous meaning" that explanation fails to capture. We infer that what a joke does is not exhausted by what it says. The same reasoning would oblige us to posit a "musical meaning" for every melody that words fail to describe.

A more economical view holds that a metaphor means just what its words mean literally—usually something plainly false or absurd—and that its power lies in what this provokes. To be told that time is a thief is to be prompted to look at time in a certain light, to notice resemblances one had not attended to, and there is no determinate end to what such noticing may turn up. The open-endedness that defeated paraphrase is, on this view, not a feature of a hidden meaning but a feature of attention, which can always be extended further.

This view is right about living metaphors and wrong about what becomes of them. Language is full of expressions that began as figures and now carry literal senses of their own: we speak of the leg of a table, the mouth of a river, the branch of a bank, the face of a cliff, without provoking anyone to notice anything. These expressions mean what they mean because a metaphorical use, repeated often enough, hardened into a convention. If metaphors only ever meant what their words meant literally, it would be a mystery how they could leave new literal meanings behind.

The two observations can be reconciled. A living metaphor has no special meaning, but it has a career. Each use invites a way of seeing; when enough speakers accept the invitation in the same way, the way of seeing becomes a sense, and the figure dies into the dictionary. Metaphor, on this account, is not a second kind of meaning but one of the routes by which the first kind is made.

## Passage 4 (Questions 13–16)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

Complaints about bureaucracy usually take the form of complaints about impersonality. The applicant who must fill in the same form as everyone else, whose circumstances are reduced to boxes and whose appeal is answered by a reference to the rules, experiences the office as a machine indifferent to the person standing in front of it. The complaint is old, and so is the classic reply: impersonality is not the defect of bureaucracy but its point. An official who treats every case by the same rule cannot favour a relative, punish an enemy or sell a decision to the highest bidder. What feels like coldness to the applicant is, from a wider vantage, a guarantee that strangers will be treated alike. Defenders of the system have long found that reply sufficient.

Both the complaint and the reply assume that impersonality is something an office either has or lacks, as though it were a property of its rules. It is better understood as an achievement, and a fragile one. Rules do not apply themselves. Every rule must be interpreted when it meets a case its authors did not foresee, and every official who interprets it brings to the task sympathies, suspicions and a sense of what the rule is for. The impersonal application of a rule is therefore not the absence of judgement but the discipline of judgement—the official's effort to set aside what she happens to feel about this applicant in favour of what any applicant in this position should receive.

That discipline is rarely complete. Studies of officials who deal directly with the public—caseworkers, inspectors, clerks at the counter—find that they routinely adapt the rules to the people before them, sometimes to help those they judge deserving, sometimes to protect themselves from those they find difficult. Much of this adaptation is invisible, because the official record shows only that the rule was applied. The office looks impersonal on paper while operating, at its edges, on something much closer to personal discretion. Officials themselves often describe these adjustments not as departures from the rules but as the only way of making the rules work.

The practical question, then, is not whether discretion should exist; it will exist whatever the rules say. The question is where it should sit and whether it can be seen. Discretion exercised openly, by officials required to record their reasons, can be reviewed and corrected, and its patterns can reveal where the rules themselves are failing. Discretion exercised covertly, under the appearance of mechanical application, escapes review precisely because it is denied.

This suggests an unexpected conclusion. The demand for strict impersonality, pressed too hard, may produce less of the equality it was meant to secure. An office forbidden to acknowledge judgement does not thereby eliminate it; it drives judgement into the places where no one can check it. Equality, on this view, is better served by judgement that is visible than by judgement that is forbidden.
