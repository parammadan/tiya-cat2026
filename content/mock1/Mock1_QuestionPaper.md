For more than a century, the zebra's coat has served as a kind of Rorschach test for biologists, each generation reading into the stripes the preoccupations of its own science. Darwin confessed himself puzzled by them and tentatively suggested sexual selection. His successors were less modest. The stripes, it was variously argued, dazzled pursuing lions, broke up the animal's outline in the shimmering heat of the savannah, cooled the body by setting up small convection currents above black and white hair, or allowed individuals to recognise one another in the herd.

Most of these proposals share a weakness that is easy to overlook: they were advanced on the strength of their plausibility rather than tested against the animal's actual circumstances. The camouflage hypothesis, for instance, assumes that a lion sees what a human sees. Yet measurements of predator vision suggest that, beyond about fifty metres in daylight, a lion cannot resolve the stripes at all; the zebra simply appears as a grey, horse-shaped object. At the distances that matter for an ambush, the pattern is invisible. Nor do the temperature studies offer much comfort. When water-filled barrels were wrapped in striped and plain hides and left in the sun, the striped barrels were not reliably cooler.

The hypothesis that has fared best was, for a long time, the least glamorous. Biting flies—horseflies and tsetse flies in particular—carry diseases that are often lethal to equids, and zebras live in regions where such flies are abundant. Field experiments in which horses were dressed in striped coats found that flies approached them as often as they approached horses in plain coats, but failed to land: they overshot, bumped into the animal or veered away at the last moment. Video analysis suggests that the stripes interfere with the visual cues flies use to control their speed on approach, rather than making the animal harder to detect in the first place. Comparative data point in the same direction: across species and subspecies of zebra, the intensity of striping tracks the regional prevalence of biting flies more closely than it tracks predator density or temperature.

None of this licenses the conclusion that the puzzle is solved. The fly hypothesis explains why stripes might be advantageous; it does not explain why they are distributed as they are across the body, why the legs of some populations are more boldly marked than their flanks, or why other animals in fly-infested regions have not converged on the same solution. A trait that originated for one reason may also be maintained for others, and it would be surprising if a feature so conspicuous carried no social meaning at all for animals that live in large, shifting herds.

What the episode illustrates is less the triumph of one theory than a change in method. The older explanations were not foolish; they were untested. Progress came not from a more ingenious story but from the patient work of asking what a striped body actually does to a lion's eye, a fly's flight path or a barrel's temperature—and from a willingness to accept answers less elegant than the questions.

## Passage 2 (Questions 5–8)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

It is a commonplace of popular history that the railway abolished local time. Before the locomotive, so the story goes, every town kept its own noon, set by the sun over its church tower; after it, a single national hour was imposed from the capital, and the rhythms of provincial life were made to answer to a timetable. The story is not false, but it has the direction of causation slightly wrong, and in its eagerness to dramatise the triumph of the machine it misses what was most interesting about the change.

To begin with, local time was never as local as the legend implies. Long before the railways, the clocks that mattered to commerce—those of the post office, the exchange, the coaching inn—were frequently set not by the sun overhead but by the time of a larger town on which local business depended. A market town forty miles from a port might keep the port's time on market days and its own on Sundays. What existed was not a mosaic of sealed temporal islands but a loose hierarchy of reference points, negotiated according to who needed to coordinate with whom.

The railway companies did not sweep this arrangement away so much as add another layer to it. Their timetables ran on the time of the line's headquarters, and for decades many stations displayed two clocks, or a single clock with two minute hands, one for the trains and one for the town. Passengers grumbled, but they also adapted with considerable ingenuity, much as modern travellers manage time zones. In many countries the legal adoption of a uniform standard came a generation or more after the railways had made it convenient, and often only after courts had been forced to decide awkward questions—whether a lease expiring at midnight expired at the town's midnight or the railway's, whether a voter arriving at a polling station at a disputed hour had arrived too late.

Seen this way, standard time was less an imposition than a ratification. The state did not invent a national hour; it chose, among several hours already in use, the one that the most powerful networks had made indispensable, and gave it the force of law. That is a subtler kind of power than the legend allows, and in some respects a more durable one. A rule that merely codifies existing practice meets little organised resistance, because those who might object to it have already, in their daily arrangements, half-accepted it.

None of this is to deny that something changed. Solar noon had a kind of public legibility: anyone could check it against the sky. Railway time could be checked only against another clock, and ultimately against an authority one could not see. The shift was not from freedom to tyranny but from a time that was, in principle, available to every observer to a time that was available only through institutions. Whether that counts as a loss depends on how much one values the ability to verify for oneself the conventions by which one's life is ordered.

## Passage 3 (Questions 9–12)

The earliest surviving recipe collections make frustrating reading for anyone who hopes to cook from them. Quantities are rarely given; times are expressed, if at all, as the length of a prayer or of a walk to the well and back; instructions end with the injunction to cook the dish until it is done. It is tempting to see in this vagueness the mark of a culinary culture that had not yet learned to measure. The more plausible reading is the opposite. These texts were not written for people who did not know how to cook. They were aide-mémoires for people who did—reminders of a combination, a sequence or a proportion that the practised cook would then adjust by eye, by smell and by the feel of dough under the palm.

The precise modern recipe, with its level teaspoons and oven temperatures, answers to a different situation: a readership that cannot be assumed to share any prior competence. Its rise tracks the growth of populations who had left the households in which cooking was learned by watching, and of publishers who needed a text that would work for a stranger in an unknown kitchen. Precision, in other words, was a technology for transmitting skill across a break in the chain of apprenticeship.

Like most such technologies, it was enormously successful and quietly costly. A recipe that specifies everything can be followed by anyone, but it also implies that following is all there is to cooking. The cook who has learned from such texts may produce a reliable cake while remaining unable to say why it rose, or what to do when the flour is different, the oven runs hot or the eggs are small. The knowledge that the old aide-mémoire took for granted—how a batter should look when it is ready—has not been transmitted; it has been designed out, replaced by instructions that make it unnecessary under standard conditions and leave the cook stranded under any others.

It would be easy to turn this into a lament for lost authenticity, but that would be a mistake of a different kind. The tacit knowledge of the traditional kitchen was also a means of exclusion: it could be acquired only by those admitted to the household, and it reproduced, along with its techniques, the hierarchies of that household. The precise written recipe democratised competence even as it thinned it. What it could not do was replace the slow education of the senses on which genuine adaptability depends.

The interesting question, then, is not whether precise recipes are better or worse than the older kind, but what each presupposes about its reader. Every instruction encodes a guess about what the person following it already knows. The more confident the guess, the shorter the text; the less confident, the longer and more explicit it must become—until, at the limit, the instructions attempt to substitute entirely for the experience they were once meant only to supplement.

## Passage 4 (Questions 13–16)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

We are inclined to believe that people should be judged only for what lies within their control. The driver who glances at the radio for a moment and runs over a child seems, on reflection, no more culpable than the driver who glances away for the same moment on an empty road; the difference between them is a matter of whether a child happened to be crossing. If blame tracks what an agent chose, and both chose the same thing, the blame should be the same. Yet almost nobody feels this way about the two drivers—including, very often, the drivers themselves.

One response is to treat the feeling as an error: an understandable but irrational tendency to let outcomes contaminate our judgement of conduct. On this view the reflective verdict is correct, and the task of moral philosophy is to bring our reactions into line with it. The difficulty is that the principle behind the verdict, applied consistently, proves ruinously expansive. Our choices are shaped by a temperament we did not select, by an upbringing we did not arrange and by circumstances that tested some of us and spared others. The citizen of a peaceful country who would, in another place and decade, have joined a persecuting mob has done nothing wrong; but neither has he done anything right. If luck must be subtracted from every assessment, it is not obvious that anything remains to be assessed.

A second response accepts the reaction and abandons the principle: outcomes simply do bear on what we are answerable for, and the unlucky driver really is worse placed morally than the lucky one. This is more faithful to experience, but it courts a different absurdity. It implies that a person's moral record can be improved or ruined by events that occur after she has done everything she will ever do—that a prudent decision can turn reckless in retrospect because the world declined to cooperate.

Perhaps the dilemma is sharp only because it assumes that "judgement" names a single thing. There is a difference between the verdict of blame, which ought to attend to what the agent did and could have known, and the burden of responsibility, which may properly fall more heavily on the one whose act ends in disaster. The unlucky driver is not more wicked than the lucky one, but it would be strange, and a little chilling, if he felt no differently. His remorse is not a confusion about his culpability; it is the recognition that his life has become entangled with a harm in a way that the other driver's has not. We would think less of him, not more, if he consoled himself with the reflection that, strictly speaking, he was no worse than anyone else who has ever glanced at a radio.

To say this is not to dissolve the problem but to relocate it. The question shifts from whether luck can affect what we deserve to how many distinct things we are doing when we hold one another to account.
