# CAT 2026 VARC — MOCK 1: ANSWER KEY AND SOLUTIONS

**Purpose:** Modern CAT baseline.
**Format:** 24 questions: 20 MCQ and 4 TITA. Time limit: 40 minutes.
**Marking:** +3 for a correct answer, −1 for a wrong MCQ, 0 for a wrong TITA.

**How the difficulty labels were assigned:** they are expert judgement, scored on five things:
- how dense and abstract the passage is
- how many reasoning steps the question needs
- how complex the question wording is
- how close the distractors are to the correct option
- how much time the question costs

They have not been checked against real candidates' responses. They describe the item, not your likely percentile.

## Passage 1: Zebra stripes (biology; accessible)

**How the passage is built:**

1. A history of the explanations offered for the stripes.
2. Those explanations were plausible but untested, and testing undercuts camouflage and cooling.
3. The fly hypothesis is the best supported, by field experiments, video analysis and comparative data.
4. Even the fly hypothesis has limits.
5. The moral: what changed was the method, not the winner.

### Q1. Answer: C — main idea (moderate)

**Passage evidence:**
- "None of this licenses the conclusion that the puzzle is solved." (P4)
- "What the episode illustrates is less the triumph of one theory than a change in method." (P5)

**Why C:** C captures both halves of the conclusion. Testing replaced plausibility, and that "narrowed the field": camouflage and cooling are undercut, and flies are best supported. But it did not "close the question".

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A) Overstatement and wrong focus.** "Best-supported" is accurate, which is why A tempts. But "comparative evidence can settle what plausibility cannot" is wrong twice. The author says the puzzle is not settled, and the decisive shift was testing in general (vision measurements, barrels, field experiments), not comparative evidence in particular.
- **(B) Part for whole.** The lion-vision finding is one result from P2. The passage is about method and the overall state of the evidence.
- **(D) Misreads the opening hook.** The Rorschach image is a framing device. The author's actual verdict is that the old explanations "were not foolish; they were untested". D turns a rhetorical opening into the thesis.

**Learning takeaway:** an option can be right about a detail ("best-supported") and still wrong as a main idea. Check every clause against the conclusion.

### Q2. Answer: B — purpose of a detail (moderate)

**Passage evidence:**
- Flies "approached them as often… but failed to land".
- The stripes "interfere with the visual cues flies use to control their speed on approach, rather than making the animal harder to detect".

**Why B:** Flies found the striped horses just as often, so the stripes do not stop detection. The effect must lie in the approach and landing. B states this.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A) Reversal.** Equal approach rates might seem to show the stripes give no protection. But the flies "failed to land", and protection lies in landing, not approaching.
- **(C) Misattribution.** The point about resolving stripes only at close range belongs to lions (P2). Nothing says flies cannot make out the pattern. The passage implies the stripes act on flies' vision.
- **(D) Unsupported.** The comparative data "point in the same direction". They corroborate the experiments; they do not rescue an inconclusive result.

**Learning takeaway:** for "primarily in order to" questions, read the sentence after the detail. It usually states the detail's job.

### Q3. Answer: D — EXCEPT (easy)

**Passage evidence:** P4 says the hypothesis "does not explain why they are distributed as they are across the body… or why other animals… have not converged", and adds that stripes "may also be maintained for others [reasons]… social meaning".

**Why D:** The equal-approach finding is not something the fly hypothesis fails to explain. The hypothesis explains it: flies approach but cannot land.

**Why the others are listed limitations:**
- **(A)** P4: distribution across the body.
- **(B)** P4: why other animals have not converged.
- **(C)** P4: maintenance for other reasons, such as social meaning.

**Distractor mechanism:** true but irrelevant, recast as a weakness.

### Q4. Answer: B — weaken (difficult)

**The claim under attack:** striping "tracks the regional prevalence of biting flies *more closely than* it tracks predator density or temperature".

**Why B:** The evidence is a comparison: flies predict striping better than the alternatives. If corrected data show predator density predicting it just as well, the comparative edge disappears, and with it the support.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A) Wrong target.** Mosquito bites concern the field experiments and perhaps the visual mechanism, not the cross-population comparison. A is also the longest and most technical option, which can make it look important.
- **(C) Strengthens a different point.** Poorer lion vision at dusk further damages the camouflage hypothesis. It does nothing to the comparison.
- **(D) Out of scope, and already conceded.** The comparison is "across species and subspecies of zebra", so antelopes fall outside it. The author already lists other animals' lack of stripes as an open question in P4.

**Learning takeaway:** if the claim is comparative ("more closely than"), the best weakener erases the comparison, not the subject in general.

## Passage 2: Railway time (social history; moderate)

**How the passage is built:**

1. The legend: railways imposed a single time.
2. Correction 1: local time was already a hierarchy of reference points.
3. Correction 2: railways added a layer. There were dual clocks for decades, and the law came late.
4. **The thesis:** standard time was ratification, not imposition, and ratification is a subtler, more durable kind of power.
5. Coda: something changed. Time became checkable only through institutions. Whether that is a loss is left to the reader.

### Q5. Answer: A — author's principal disagreement (difficult)

**Passage evidence:**
- The story "has the direction of causation slightly wrong".
- "standard time was less an imposition than a ratification… the one that the most powerful networks had made indispensable".

**Why A:** The principal disagreement concerns what standard time was, a ratification rather than an imposition. A states this.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B) Unsupported.** The passage shows passengers adapting, not towns resisting, and the state never "intervened on their behalf".
- **(C) Partial truth plus distortion, the strongest trap.** C echoes a real supporting point from P2: town clocks were not simply solar. But it misstates that point. The passage says clocks were set by "a larger town on which local business depended", in "a loose hierarchy of reference points", not "by the capital". And P2 opens with "To begin with", which marks it as a premise that prepares the P4 thesis, not as the principal disagreement.
- **(D) Distortion.** The courts decided awkward questions, but the author explicitly denies that the state "invent[ed] a national hour".

**Learning takeaway:** "principal" or "primary" disagreement means the thesis. Signposts like "to begin with" mark supporting points.

### Q6. Answer: D — purpose of an example (easy)

**Passage evidence:** "did not sweep this arrangement away so much as add another layer to it… for decades many stations displayed two clocks".

**Why D:** "Layered on top of rather than substituted for" paraphrases "add another layer… [not] sweep away".

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A) Unsupported cause.** The second clock is never attributed to passenger resistance. Passengers "grumbled" and "adapted".
- **(B) Overreach.** The railways made a standard "convenient" and one hour "indispensable", so their influence went well beyond the stations. The author corrects the mechanism of that influence, not its size.
- **(C) Wrong claim illustrated.** The two-clock example shows coexistence. It says nothing about town clocks being "abandoned gradually", which the passage never describes.

### Q7. Answer: A — assumption (difficult)

**The argument:**
- Premise: a codifying rule "meets little organised resistance".
- Conclusion: so ratification is "in some respects a more durable" form of power.

**The gap:** the premise is about resistance and the conclusion is about durability. The bridge is the assumption that a lack of organised opposition makes a rule harder to dislodge. That is A.

**Negation test:** suppose opposition had no bearing on how hard a rule is to dislodge. The premise would then no longer support "more durable", so A is required.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B) Irrelevant comparison.** The argument never ranks the strength of the state against the networks.
- **(C) Stronger than needed.** The argument needs only that ratified rules are more durable "in some respects". It does not need imposed rules to be "usually reversed". Negate C and the argument survives.
- **(D) Beyond the argument.** Half-acceptance is enough. Full endorsement is never required.

**Learning takeaway:** an assumption is the minimum bridge between premise and conclusion. Options that say more than the argument needs (C, D) are classic traps.

### Q8. Answer: C — stance (moderate)

**Passage evidence:**
- "None of this is to deny that something changed."
- "The shift was not from freedom to tyranny."
- "Whether that counts as a loss depends on how much one values…"

**Why C:** The author identifies a real shift: verification became possible only through institutions. Whether that is a loss is explicitly left to the reader's values.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A) Too emotional.** Nothing expresses regret, and "not from freedom to tyranny" rules out nostalgia for freedom.
- **(B) Too settled.** The author does not call the change a "reasonable price". The cost–benefit verdict is left open.
- **(D) Overreach.** "An authority one could not see" concerns who can verify the time. The passage never suggests abuse.

**Learning takeaway:** when an author ends with "whether X depends on…", choose the stance that withholds the verdict.

## Passage 3: Recipes and tacit knowledge (cultural history; moderate)

**How the passage is built:**

1. Vague early recipes were reminders for experts.
2. Precise recipes transmitted skill once apprenticeship broke down.
3. The cost: knowledge was "designed out", leaving cooks stranded.
4. The counterweight: tacit knowledge excluded people, while precise recipes democratised competence but thinned it.
5. The real question is what each kind of text presupposes about its reader.

### Q9. Answer: B — detail / explanation (easy)

**Passage evidence:** "aide-mémoires for people who did [know how to cook]… that the practised cook would then adjust by eye, by smell…"

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** This is the "tempting" reading that the author rejects outright: "The more plausible reading is the opposite."
- **(C) Fusion.** It welds P4's exclusion theme onto an invented motive, the deliberate omission of quantities.
- **(D) Plausible but unstated attitude.** The passage never mentions valuing variation.

### Q10. Answer: D — analogy (moderate)

**What the analogy must match:**

| The precise recipe (passage) | Option D |
|---|---|
| written for a stranger with no prior skill | a new hire |
| works "under standard conditions" | runs the machine correctly |
| leaves the user "stranded under any others" | says nothing about faults |

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A)** This is the analogy for the old aide-mémoire, a reminder for experts.
- **(B)** This is the analogy for tacit, exclusive household knowledge.
- **(C) A different failure mode.** C fails because it becomes obsolete. The precise recipe does not go out of date; it fails only outside standard conditions.

**Learning takeaway:** list two or three structural features before reading the options. A and B are faithful analogies to the *other* items in the passage.

### Q11. Answer: B — EXCEPT (easy)

**Passage evidence:** the knowledge "has not been transmitted; it has been designed out".

**Why B:** B says the opposite of the passage.

**Why the others are suggested by the passage:**
- **(A)** P4: "acquired only by those admitted to the household".
- **(C)** P4: "reproduced… the hierarchies of that household".
- **(D)** P4: "the slow education of the senses on which genuine adaptability depends". P3 makes the same point with the cook stranded by different flour or a hot oven.

### Q12. Answer: A — phrase in context (moderate)

**Passage evidence:** the gloss is in the same sentence. The knowledge was "designed out, replaced by instructions that make it unnecessary under standard conditions".

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(B) Invented agent and motive.** No publisher's judgement is described. "Designed out" describes how the text works, not anyone's decision.
- **(C) Wrong mechanism.** The knowledge is not transmitted in the first place, because of the text's design. Nothing is said about how often people cook.
- **(D) Unsupported comparison.** "More consistent results than intuitive judgement could" is never claimed. The author stresses that such cooks are stranded outside standard conditions. D tempts because precise recipes do produce "a reliable cake".

**Learning takeaway:** in vocabulary-in-context questions, the meaning is usually in the clause right next to the phrase. Read it before your own sense of the word takes over.

## Passage 4: Moral luck (moral philosophy; abstract, difficult)

**How the passage is built:**

1. The puzzle: the control principle against our reaction to the two drivers.
2. **Response 1 (cited view):** treat the reaction as an error. Applied consistently, this principle is "ruinously expansive".
3. **Response 2 (cited view):** let outcomes matter. This "courts a different absurdity": moral records changing after the fact.
4. **The author's view:** separate the *verdict of blame* (conduct and knowledge) from the *burden of responsibility* (which can be heavier after a disaster). Remorse is fitting, not a confusion.
5. The problem is "relocated", not dissolved.

**Track ownership.** Most wrong options here describe Response 1 or Response 2 accurately.

### Q13. Answer: C — purpose of an example (moderate)

**Passage evidence:** the example sits between "applied consistently, proves ruinously expansive" and "it is not obvious that anything remains to be assessed".

**Why C:** The citizen shows that the control principle, applied consistently, empties moral assessment.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A) Misattribution.** That outcomes bear on desert is Response 2's claim, not the point of this example.
- **(B) Reversal.** "Neither has he done anything right." The citizen gets no credit.
- **(D) Distortion.** The example says upbringing shapes our *choices*. It does not argue that our *reactions* are unreliable. Treating reactions as an error is Response 1's position, and this example is used against Response 1.

### Q14. Answer: D — author vs cited views (difficult)

**Passage evidence:**
- Blame "ought to attend to what the agent did and could have known".
- Responsibility "may properly fall more heavily on the one whose act ends in disaster".

**Why D:** "agents who acted alike" captures the identical conduct of the two drivers.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A) Response 1.** It also contradicts the author's line "His remorse is not a confusion about his culpability."
- **(B) Response 2.** The author says the unlucky driver "is not more wicked".
- **(C) Contradicts the final paragraph:** "not to dissolve the problem but to relocate it."

**Learning takeaway:** in "view 1, view 2, my view" passages, label each paragraph's owner as you read.

### Q15. Answer: C — double-negative inference (difficult)

**Decode the question first:** "None… can be inferred EXCEPT" means three options cannot be inferred and one can. Find the one supported option.

**Why C:** "His remorse is not a confusion about his culpability." Denying that his remorse *must* rest on an error is directly supported.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A) Contradicted.** For the author, blame tracks "what the agent did and could have known". The lucky driver did the same thing, so he is no less blameworthy. The reason A gives (no harm resulted) is outcome-based, which is exactly what the author's blame verdict excludes.
- **(B) Misattribution.** This is Response 1 ("On this view…"), a position the author goes on to criticise.
- **(D) True premise, unsupported ranking.** The author does call the second response "more faithful to experience". But the author then says it "courts a different absurdity" and sets both responses aside in P4. D turns one conceded advantage into an overall verdict.

**Learning takeaway:** rewrite double-negative questions ("Which ONE can be inferred?") before reading the options. Also watch for options that quote a real concession and then overextend it, as D does.

### Q16. Answer: A — application (difficult)

**The scenario must show two things together:**
1. The **same blame** for the same conduct.
2. A **different burden of responsibility** for the one whose act ended in disaster.

| Option | Same conduct? | Same blame? | Heavier burden on the one whose act ended in disaster? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Yes | Yes | Yes (feels bound to meet the family) | **Matches** |
| B | Yes | No (outcome decides the censure) | — | Response 2 |
| C | No (careful vs careless) | — | No disaster | Different contrast |
| D | Yes | Yes | No (both treat it as closed) | Missing half |

**Why D is the strongest trap:** it gets the blame half exactly right. But the author says it would be "strange, and a little chilling" if the unlucky one "felt no differently", and that "we would think less of him" for consoling himself that way. D is the attitude the author criticises.

**Learning takeaway:** when a distinction has two parts, check that the option shows *both*. Close distractors usually get one part right.

## Verbal ability

### Q17. Answer: 2143 — para jumble (moderate)

**Correct order:**
1. **2** introduces commonplace books. Every other sentence depends on it ("The practice", "such a notebook", "one heading or another").
2. **1** says the practice was "*however*, less passive than it sounds". This contrasts with the mechanical-sounding "copied" in 2.
3. **4** gives the reason: choosing a heading was already a judgement.
4. **3** concludes: "Over the years, *then*…"

**Rejected orders:**
- **2413:** 4 has already shown the practice involves judgement, so 1's "however… less passive" has nothing left to contrast with.
- **2134:** "then" in 3 draws the conclusion before its reason, and 4 is left dangling.
- **Any other opener:** 1, 3 and 4 all need something earlier to refer back to.

### Q18. Answer: 3421 — para jumble (moderate)

**Correct order:**
1. **3** describes the event: the bridge sways.
2. **4**: "*Its* engineers" refers back to the bridge in 3.
3. **2**: "*these* sideways pushes" refers back to 4's push. Ordinarily the pushes cancel out.
4. **1**: "*But* once the deck began to move…" contrasts with 2's "ordinarily".

**Locked links:**
- 4 must come before 2, because "these" needs a referent.
- 2 must come before 1, because "But" needs the "ordinarily" to contrast with.
- 4 cannot open, because "Its" needs a noun before it.

So 3421 is the only valid order.

### Q19. Answer: 2 — odd sentence out (moderate)

**The coherent paragraph is 3-1-5-4:**
- **3:** strangers assemble queues spontaneously.
- **1:** the queue's unwritten rules are enforced intensely.
- **5:** what it enforces is fairness by arrival, not efficiency.
- **4:** "That is why" cutting in angers people more than an equal delay.

**Why 2 is odd:** Sentence 2 is about queues, but it treats a queue as a *signal*: a long line advertises quality and grows itself. The paragraph's subject is the queue as an unwritten norm of fairness. Nothing in 2 connects to "rules", "fairness" or "cutting in". It also cannot be slotted in:
- 1's "Its rules" must follow 3. Placed between them, 2 would make "Its" refer to the restaurant.
- 4's "That is why" needs 5 directly before it.

**Learning takeaway:** ask what the paragraph claims, not just what it is about.

### Q20. Answer: 3 — odd sentence out (easy)

**The coherent paragraph is 4-5-1-2.** Swapping 5 and 1 also works and does not change the answer.

**Why 3 is odd:** the other four describe how the dance works, from performance to response. Sentence 3 is about how scientists received the discovery.

### Q21. Answer: B — para summary (moderate)

**What the summary must keep:**
- the shift from concealment to reversible, detectable repair
- the change being one of honesty, not skill

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A) Misreads "interpretations date".** That means interpretations go out of fashion. It does not mean the old retouching physically deteriorated, which is an invented cause.
- **(C) Close but wrong.** The paragraph does say restorers no longer pretend the damage "never happened". But restorers have not "given up retouching". They still retouch, in a way that is invisible at normal viewing distance and detectable only up close. C turns *detectable* repair into *no* repair.
- **(D) Contradicted twice.** "No viewer can detect" contradicts "can be detected on close inspection", and "improved techniques" contradicts "less the restorer's skill".

### Q22. Answer: D — para summary (moderate)

**Why D:** It has the mechanism (only the sense that mattered at contact is borrowed) and the conclusion (loanwords map what speakers needed words for, and from whom, its "sources").

**Why the others are wrong:**
- **(A) Wrong timing.** The narrowing happens *at* borrowing ("fix a word to whatever situation first brought speakers… together"). It is not gradual drift afterwards.
- **(B) Overgeneralises.** "Which cultures shaped it most deeply" is a claim about overall influence. The paragraph claims only "a rough record of what… speakers needed words for, and from whom, at particular moments". B sounds measured, which makes it a strong trap.
- **(C) Misreads the metaphor.** Amber preserves "a narrow slice", not the full original meaning. The paragraph's first sentence says a language "rarely borrows the word's full range of meaning", which contradicts C.

**Learning takeaway:** pin down exactly what a metaphor preserves. Summary distractors often stretch it.

### Q23. Answer: C — gap 3 (moderate)

**What the sentence needs:** "*On this view*… not choosing a better channel… they are shouting" requires the by-product-of-loudness view to come directly before it.

| Gap | Fits? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No | No view has been stated yet. |
| 2 | No | It would follow the noise explanation, which says birds *are* choosing a better channel, so "this view" would contradict its own referent. |
| **3** | **Yes** | It follows "a by-product of singing louder" and restates that view vividly. "If so…" then continues naturally. |
| 4 | No | The paragraph has already moved on to the consequences, so going back to restate the view breaks the progression. |

### Q24. Answer: D — gap 4 (easy)

**What the sentence needs:** "Deception, *in this account*, was never the active ingredient; it merely accompanied *the things that were*." It requires an account of what does work.

| Gap | Fits? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | No | No explanation has been offered yet. |
| 3 | No (the main rival) | The surprising result has been reported, but "the things that were" has no referent until the ritual list appears. |
| **4** | **Yes** | It follows the ritual list (consultation, prescription, routine). "The things that were" refers to that list. |

## Diagnostic guide

| If you missed… | Work on |
|---|---|
| Q1, Q5 | Telling the thesis apart from supporting points ("to begin with", "less X than Y") |
| Q4, Q7 | Isolating the exact claim; the negation test for assumptions |
| Q13–Q16 | Tracking whether a statement is the author's view or a cited one |
| Q15 | Decoding double negatives before reading the options |
| Q10, Q16 | Mapping features in analogy and application questions; checking both halves of a distinction |
| Q19, Q22 | A paragraph's claim versus its topic; reading metaphors precisely |
| Q17, Q18 | Locking chains of connectors and demonstratives |

**Pacing benchmark used in design:** about 26–28 minutes for RC (about 2.5 minutes to read each passage, then about 1 minute per question) and about 12–14 minutes for VA. Under time pressure, take the fast marks first: Q3, Q9, Q11, Q20, Q24.
