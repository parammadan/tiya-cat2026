## Passage 1 (Questions 1–4)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

Few historical theses have been demolished as thoroughly, or have survived as stubbornly, as the claim that childhood was invented. Its original proponent, a historian working largely from paintings, diaries and manuals of conduct, argued that medieval Europe had no concept of childhood as a distinct stage of life: once past infancy, children were dressed as small adults, mixed freely in adult work and play, and were mourned, when they died, with a restraint that later generations would find shocking. The modern sentiment of childhood—the conviction that children are fragile, innocent and in need of protected time—emerged, on this view, only in the early modern period.

The critics' case is now familiar. Medieval sources, read more widely, are full of toys, of laws treating children differently, of parents grieving extravagantly for the children they had lost. Painting is a poor guide to perception, because artistic conventions for depicting bodies change for reasons unrelated to how actual children were seen. And muted expressions of grief in a world of high infant mortality may say more about the genres in which grief was recorded than about the grief itself. Each objection is sound, and together they dispose of the thesis in its strong form: medieval people plainly knew that children were different, and they loved them.

What is less often noticed is that the critics answered a question the thesis did not most need to ask. Whether medieval parents loved their children is a question about sentiment, and sentiment is notoriously hard to read off from documents. The more defensible claim concerns institutions: the steady extension of the period of life during which a person is excluded from work and adult responsibility and placed instead in specialised settings—schools above all—organised around the idea that this period has a value of its own. That extension is not in dispute, and it is precisely the kind of change that can alter what childhood is, even if it does not alter how much children are loved.

Read this way, the original argument made two claims, one about feeling and one about arrangement, and presented them as though they were the same. Its critics showed the first to be false and assumed that they had thereby refuted the second. But a society can cherish its children while expecting them to herd animals at seven, and another can schedule every hour of a child's day around its development while being, in sentiment, no more tender. It is the arrangement, not the feeling, that has a history in the strong sense.

None of this rehabilitates the original evidence, much of which was as weak as the critics alleged. It suggests rather that a thesis can be wrong in the form its author gave it and right about something its author did not quite isolate—and that the refutation of the first does not, by itself, settle the second.

## Passage 2 (Questions 5–8)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

When remote work became widespread, forecasts of the city's decline followed quickly. If the office was no longer necessary, the argument ran, neither was the expensive apartment within commuting distance of it, and the logic that had drawn people into dense centres for two centuries would run in reverse. The forecasts were not foolish, but they rested on a narrow account of what cities are for. They treated the city as a device for assembling workers in one place so that they could be supervised and coordinated—an account on which a technology that coordinates them at a distance does indeed make the city redundant.

Economists who study cities have long emphasised a different set of functions. Density makes it cheaper to find the right job, the right employee, the right supplier or the right collaborator, because the pool of candidates within reach is larger. It also accelerates learning: workers in large cities appear to acquire skills faster than comparable workers elsewhere, and they keep much of that advantage when they move away, which suggests that the city is not merely rewarding skills people already have but helping them acquire new ones. Neither function depends on a daily commute to a fixed desk. Both depend on a kind of unplanned proximity that video calls reproduce poorly, because calls are scheduled, bounded and confined to people one has already decided to talk to.

This suggests that remote work subtracts some of the city's functions more than others. It weakens the case for living near the office, but it leaves largely intact the case for living near other people who do what one does or would like to do. The effect should therefore fall unevenly. Established workers, whose networks are formed and whose skills are largely acquired, gain most from the freedom to leave. Younger workers, for whom the city's value lies chiefly in the learning and matching it provides, have the least reason to go—and some reason, as rents fall, to arrive.

If this is right, the likely outcome is neither the death of the city nor a return to the status quo, but a re-sorting of who lives there and why. Such a re-sorting could prove more disruptive than the forecasts of decline imagined, for a city whose population grows younger and more transient has different fiscal needs and a different political temper from one whose residents are rooted. Commercial districts built around daily commuters face a genuine crisis even in a city that is otherwise thriving.

The difficulty for anyone predicting what will happen is that the learning advantage of cities is easier to measure than to explain. If we knew precisely which kinds of encounter produce it, we could say which kinds remote work can replace. As it is, we know that density helps people learn; we do not know how much of that help can travel down a cable.

## Passage 3 (Questions 9–12)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

When a celebrated finding in psychology fails to replicate, two interpretations immediately present themselves. On the first, the original result was a false positive—an artefact of small samples, flexible analysis and the preference of journals for surprising claims—and the failure to replicate simply reveals that there was never anything there. On the second, the effect is real but fragile: it depends on conditions that the original study happened to satisfy and the replication did not, such as the population tested, the wording of instructions or the cultural moment. Defenders of a finding naturally prefer the second interpretation, and critics have learned to regard it with suspicion.

The suspicion is warranted, but not for the reason usually given. The objection to the "hidden moderator" defence is not that moderators are implausible. Most effects in psychology surely do depend on context; it would be strange if they did not. The objection is that a moderator invoked only after a replication has failed can rescue any finding whatever. Indeed, the more plausible a moderator sounds, the more tempting it is to invoke it after the fact, which is exactly why plausibility cannot be the test. If every failure can be attributed to some unspecified difference between studies, no failure counts against the claim, and a claim against which nothing can count has ceased to say anything about the world.

The remedy is not to forbid appeals to context but to require that they be made in advance. A researcher who states, before the replication, the conditions under which the effect should and should not appear has made a claim that the replication can test. If the effect then fails to appear under conditions the researcher said were favourable, the failure is informative in a way it could not otherwise be. Stated like this, the requirement is less a rule about replication than a rule about what it means to have made a claim in the first place.

It follows that much of the debate about whether particular findings are "real" is premature. Many original papers specified their effects so loosely that neither a successful nor a failed replication could bear on them very much. A successful replication of a vague claim confirms little, since a vague claim is compatible with many outcomes; a failed one refutes little, for the same reason. What the replication crisis has exposed, on this reading, is less a surplus of false findings than a shortage of precise ones.

This does not mean that every failed replication is uninformative. Where the original authors did specify their conditions, and the replication honoured them, failure is about as damning as evidence in this field gets. Where they did not, the most a failure can do is invite the authors to say, now and in advance, what their claim actually was. But the most important lesson may be prospective rather than retrospective: not which old findings to discard, but how new ones should be stated.

## Passage 4 (Questions 13–16)

*The passage below is accompanied by a set of questions. Choose the best answer to each question.*

For much of the twentieth century, the origin of farming was described as a revolution. Within a few centuries, on this account, foragers in a handful of regions began sowing and harvesting wild cereals, unwittingly selected plants whose seeds stayed on the stalk rather than scattering, and soon found themselves sedentary, numerous and dependent on crops that could no longer reproduce without human help. The word conveyed both speed and rupture: a before and an after, separated by a threshold that, once crossed, could not be recrossed.

Excavations over the past few decades have made the speed hard to sustain. Plant remains from sites in south-west Asia show people cultivating wild forms of cereals for a thousand years or more before the domestic, non-shattering forms came to dominate their harvests. Domestic traits appear first in small proportions and spread slowly, sometimes receding before advancing again. The pattern differs from region to region, and in some places domestic forms never came to dominate at all before cultivation was given up. At several sites, people seem to have farmed, abandoned farming and returned to it. If there was a threshold, it was approached by a long and wandering path.

Some revisionists have drawn the further conclusion that the idea of a revolution should be abandoned altogether—that what happened was not a transformation but a drift, a gradual adjustment of foraging practices with no decisive moment. This goes further than the evidence warrants. The slowness of the biological process tells us how long it took for crops to change. It does not tell us how quickly human societies changed once they had come to rely on those crops. The two timescales need not match. A dependence that was centuries in the making might nonetheless, once established, have reorganised settlement, labour, property and population within a few generations. Sedentary villages, once they exist, have a momentum of their own: they accumulate stores, invite raids, demand defence and make it harder for anyone to leave.

What the evidence actually requires, then, is a distinction between the process by which farming emerged and the consequences that followed from it. The process was slow, reversible and in many places tentative; there was no moment at which anyone decided to become a farmer. The consequences may well have been abrupt, and in that sense the older vocabulary captured something real, even if it misdated it. It is not that the older writers saw a sudden event where there was none; it is that they attached the suddenness to the wrong part of the story. A revolution need not be quick in its causes to be revolutionary in its effects.

The point is not merely terminological. If the consequences were abrupt while the process was slow, the people who lived through the change would have experienced it as sudden, whatever its long prehistory—much as a coastline that has been eroding for centuries can still collapse in a single storm.
