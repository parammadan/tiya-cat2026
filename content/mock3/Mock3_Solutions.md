# CAT 2026 VARC — MOCK 3: ANSWER KEY AND SOLUTIONS

**Purpose:** close-option discrimination and inference. Most questions here have one strong competing option. The skill being trained is spotting the single qualifier, scope or attribution that sinks it.

**Format:** 24 questions in 40 minutes. 21 are MCQ and 3 are TITA.
- **Verbal Ability:** 3 para jumbles, 2 sentence placements and 3 para summaries. This is the CAT 2022 VA mix.
- **Marking:** +3 for a correct answer, −1 for a wrong MCQ, 0 for a wrong TITA.

**Mechanisms emphasised:**

| Mechanism | Questions |
|---|---|
| Separating the author's view from the views the author cites | Q1, Q2, Q9, Q13, Q16 |
| Assumptions | Q2, Q5 |
| Qualifiers ("proves" vs "tells against"; "only"; "may have") | Q6, Q10, Q16 |
| Reasoning across paragraphs | Q3, Q7, Q15 |
| Weakening | Q11 |
| Double negatives | Q10 |

**About the difficulty labels:** they are expert judgement for a well-prepared candidate. They have not been validated against real candidate responses.

## Passage 1: Was childhood invented? (history; difficult)

**How the passage is built:**
1. **The thesis:** medieval Europe had no concept of childhood; the modern sentiment of childhood arose in the early modern period.
2. **The critics:** their objections are sound and refute the *strong* form of the thesis. Medieval people knew children were different, and loved them.
3. **The author's turn:** the critics answered the wrong question. The defensible claim is *institutional*: the extension of a protected period of life spent in schools.
4. **The thesis conflated two claims**, one about feeling and one about arrangement. The critics refuted the first and assumed they had refuted the second.
5. **The original evidence stays weak.** A thesis can be wrong in its stated form yet right about something it did not isolate.

### Q1. Answer: C (author vs cited thesis; difficult)

**Evidence:** "the original argument made two claims, one about feeling and one about arrangement, and presented them as though they were the same. Its critics showed the first to be false…"

**Why C is right:** the thesis is refuted on feeling and survives on arrangement. "Conflated" corresponds to "presented them as though they were the same".

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(D) Strongest rival.** It says the thesis's "central claim was always institutional", so the critics were "beside the point". But the author says the institutional claim is something the original author "did not quite isolate". It wasn't the thesis's central claim. The critics weren't beside the point either: they refuted the claim the thesis actually made.
- **(A)** Contradicts "right about something its author did not quite isolate".
- **(B)** A reversal. The author calls the critics' objections "sound" and the original evidence "as weak as the critics alleged".

**Takeaway:** "the thesis was really about X" and "the thesis contained X without isolating it" are different claims. CAT distractors exploit exactly that gap.

### Q2. Answer: A (critics' faulty assumption; difficult)

**Evidence:** "Its critics showed the first to be false and assumed that they had thereby refuted the second."

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(B)** The critics argued the opposite: genres distort the recorded grief ("may say more about the genres…"). This assumption cannot be theirs.
- **(C)** A reversal of the critics' view that "Painting is a poor guide to perception".
- **(D)** True of the original historian, but it isn't an *assumption* in the critics' reasoning. It is a fact they used. True but irrelevant.

### Q3. Answer: B (relevant evidence across paragraphs; difficult)

**The defensible claim:** "the steady extension of the period of life during which a person is excluded from work". Rising ages for starting full-time work over centuries are direct evidence of that extension.

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A)** Evidence about *sentiment*, the claim the author says the critics refuted.
- **(C)** Paintings, which "change for reasons unrelated to how actual children were seen". They are unreliable, and at most bear on perception.
- **(D) Strongest rival.** Laws *are* institutions, which makes D tempting. But D shows only that medieval society treated children differently. That is the critics' evidence against the *strong* thesis. It says nothing about the *extension over time* of a protected period.

**Takeaway:** match the evidence to the precise claim. Here the claim is about change over time in an arrangement, not the mere existence of an arrangement.

### Q4. Answer: B (purpose of an example; moderate)

**Evidence:** the remark sits in a sentence pairing two hypothetical societies: one cherishes its children but sets them to work early; the other organises children's time but is "no more tender". The point is that sentiment and arrangement come apart.

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A)** No comparison of how much children were loved is made.
- **(C)** An unsupported value judgement.
- **(D)** A misattribution. "Treated as small adults" is the *thesis's* claim, which the critics denied.

## Passage 2: Remote work and the city (urban economics; moderate)

**How the passage is built:**
1. **The forecasts of decline** rest on a narrow view: the city as a place where workers are assembled and coordinated.
2. **Two other functions of cities**, matching and learning, depend on unplanned proximity that video calls reproduce poorly.
3. **The effects will be uneven:** established workers leave, younger workers stay or arrive.
4. **The result is a re-sorting**, not a death. It may still be disruptive, and commercial districts face a crisis.
5. **The learning mechanism is not understood**, so the prediction is uncertain.

### Q5. Answer: D (assumption of a cited argument; difficult)

**Evidence:** the forecasts "treated the city as a device for assembling workers in one place so that they could be supervised and coordinated". D states this assumption.

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A)** Not needed. Decline could follow even if adoption were uneven.
- **(B) Strongest rival.** It is tempting because the author criticises the forecasts for ignoring the benefits of proximity. But the forecasts didn't *assume* video replaces proximity. They simply didn't count proximity among the city's functions. Ignoring a factor and assuming a claim about it are different things.
- **(C)** A possible consequence of the forecast, not an assumption behind it.

### Q6. Answer: B (inference with qualifiers; difficult)

**Evidence:** "which suggests that the city is not merely rewarding skills people already have but helping them acquire new ones."

**Why B is right:** "Tells against the view that cities *do no more than* reward" matches "not *merely* rewarding".

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A) Strongest rival.** "Proves" is too strong, since the passage says only "suggests". "Rather than attracting" is an either/or the passage avoids: "not merely" allows that cities do both.
- **(C)** A misreading. The finding concerns skills kept after moving away. It says nothing about whether the learning advantage can be delivered at a distance.
- **(D)** An invented expectation ("more slowly than was expected").

**Takeaway:** compare the force of each qualifier: suggests/proves, not merely/rather than.

### Q7. Answer: A (prediction; moderate)

**Evidence:** younger workers "have the least reason to go", and established workers "gain most from the freedom to leave". If early-career workers left *faster*, the author's account would be contradicted.

**Why the other options are wrong:** B, C and D are each predicted in paragraphs 3–4. B: commercial districts "face a genuine crisis even in a city that is otherwise thriving". C: "some reason, as rents fall, to arrive". D: established workers gain "freedom to leave".

### Q8. Answer: D (function of the conclusion; moderate)

**Evidence:** "the learning advantage of cities is easier to measure than to explain… we do not know how much of that help can travel down a cable."

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A)** Overreach. The author flags uncertainty but does not abandon the argument.
- **(B)** A misreading. An advantage that is "easier to measure than to explain" is measured. Being hard to explain does not mean it is overstated.
- **(C)** Unsupported. The author says we *don't know*.

## Passage 3: What failed replications show (philosophy of science; difficult)

**How the passage is built:**
1. **Two interpretations of a failed replication:** the original was a false positive, or the effect is fragile.
2. **The hidden-moderator defence** is suspect. This is not because moderators are implausible, but because invoking them *after the fact* can rescue anything.
3. **The remedy:** state conditions in advance. This is really a rule about what it is to make a claim.
4. **Vague claims can be neither confirmed nor refuted.** The crisis shows a shortage of *precise* findings rather than a surplus of false ones.
5. **Some failures do count.** The main lesson concerns how future findings are stated.

### Q9. Answer: C (author vs critics; moderate)

**Evidence:** "The suspicion is warranted, but not for the reason usually given… not that moderators are implausible… a moderator invoked only after a replication has failed can rescue any finding."

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A)** A reversal. "Most effects… surely do depend on context."
- **(B) Close hedged trap.** It sounds reasonable: allow moderators only when they are independently plausible. But the author says plausibility "cannot be the test", because "the more plausible a moderator sounds, the more tempting it is to invoke it after the fact". The test is *timing*: the moderator must be stated in advance.
- **(D)** The author's own conclusion is "less a surplus of false findings".

### Q10. Answer: A (double-negative inference; very difficult)

**Decode the stem:** exactly one option *does* follow from the argument.

**Why A follows:** "a failed one refutes little, for the same reason" (paragraph 4).

**Why the other options don't follow:**
- **(B) "Only" overreaches.** The author says failures are *most* damning where conditions were specified. That doesn't mean other replications shouldn't be attempted. A failed replication can still prompt authors to "say… what their claim actually was".
- **(C)** Contradicts "less a surplus of false findings".
- **(D)** An unsupported comparison. The passage never ranks success against failure for *precise* claims. It discusses vague claims, where both are uninformative.

### Q11. Answer: D (weaken a comparative claim; very difficult)

**The claim:** the crisis reveals "less a surplus of false findings than a shortage of precise ones". In other words, imprecision explains the failures better than falsity does.

**Why D weakens it:** if precise findings with advance conditions fail faithful replications *as often as* vague ones, then imprecision isn't what explains the failures. The findings are simply false. That revives the "surplus of false findings" reading.

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A)** Sceptical replicators raise a separate worry about bias. It doesn't distinguish falsity from imprecision.
- **(B)** About future reform. Irrelevant to what the crisis has exposed.
- **(C)** Supports the author: psychology's claims are especially vague.

**Takeaway:** to weaken "less X than Y", find evidence that Y (here, imprecision) doesn't explain the outcome.

### Q12. Answer: C (purpose of a statement; moderate)

**Evidence:** the line concludes the argument that "a moderator invoked only after a replication has failed can rescue any finding whatever."

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A)** Overgeneralises. Context-dependent claims stated in advance are testable, which is the author's own remedy.
- **(B)** A scope shift.
- **(D)** A reversal. The author's point warns against dismissing failures *too easily*, not too quickly.

## Passage 4: Was farming a revolution? (archaeology; moderate–difficult)

**How the passage is built:**
1. **The old account:** a fast revolution.
2. **The evidence:** domestication was slow, uneven and reversible.
3. **The revisionists over-infer.** Biological slowness says nothing about the speed of social change, and settled villages have their own momentum.
4. **Distinguish the process from its consequences.** The process was slow, but the consequences may have been abrupt. The old account was right about suddenness but attached it to the wrong part of the story.
5. **The change was experienced as sudden**, as in the coastline analogy.

### Q13. Answer: B (author vs revisionists; difficult)

**Evidence:** "The slowness of the biological process tells us how long it took for crops to change. It does not tell us how quickly human societies changed."

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A)** A reversal. The revisionists rely on that very evidence.
- **(C)** Invented. No dating dispute is described.
- **(D)** A misreading. "Not merely terminological" is the *author's* point in the last paragraph, and isn't attributed to the revisionists.

### Q14. Answer: A (purpose of an analogy; easy)

**Evidence:** "the people who lived through the change would have experienced it as sudden, whatever its long prehistory."

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(B)** Unsupported. The slow process (erosion) is plainly recognisable in the analogy. The point is about how the change is *experienced*, not whether it can be detected.
- **(C)** A reversal. The point is that the change *was* felt.
- **(D)** Invented claim about memory.

### Q15. Answer: D (inference across paragraphs; moderate)

**Evidence:** the author's distinction predicts that consequences were rapid *once* dependence was established ("within a few generations", "momentum of their own"). Rapid village growth after full domestication is exactly what it predicts.

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A)** A reversal. The old account was wrong about the *speed* of domestication and possibly right about its consequences.
- **(B)** The revisionist inference the author rejects. Abandonment shows that the process was reversible, not that its consequences couldn't be abrupt.
- **(C) Hedged but contradicted.** The final paragraph says the people who lived through the change would have experienced it as sudden "whatever its long prehistory". A slow process does not imply a gradual experience.

### Q16. Answer: C (qualified evaluation; difficult)

**Evidence:**
- "The consequences *may well have been* abrupt."
- "The older vocabulary captured something real, even if it misdated it."
- "They attached the suddenness to the wrong part of the story."

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A) Strongest rival.** It says the old account described "both how farming emerged and what followed". But the old account was wrong about *how farming emerged*: it claimed speed where the process was slow. "Apart from its dates" hides that error.
- **(B)** A reversal. The old account was right about the *character* of the change and wrong about its timing.
- **(D)** Contradicts paragraph 2.

**Takeaway:** keep the author's hedge. C's "may have" matches "may well have been". A claims far more than the author does.

## Verbal Ability

### Q17. Answer: 3241 (para jumble; moderate)

**Correct order:**
1. **3** introduces the ice core as a record.
2. **2** gives the mechanism: compressed snow traps bubbles.
3. **4** adds a complication: "however", air keeps moving through the upper layers.
4. **1** concludes: "therefore" the bubble is younger than the ice.

**Why not other orders:**
- 1 needs both 2's bubbles and 4's mechanism before it. (*Audit note:* sentence 4 now says air moves "for decades, and at some sites for centuries", so it agrees with 1's "sometimes by centuries".)
- 4's "the record" needs 3 before it.
- 3-4-2-1 interrupts the complication with the basic mechanism.

### Q18. Answer: 2314 (para jumble; moderate)

**Correct order:**
1. **2** states the problem: reading at speed, at distance, at a glance.
2. **3** gives concrete failures: letters merge, typefaces blur.
3. **1** "Because a page or a monitor cannot reproduce such conditions" introduces testing on tracks. "A page or a monitor" refers back to 3.
4. **4** "What matters, it turns out" gives the finding from those tests.

**Why not other orders:**
- 2-3-4-1 puts the finding ("it turns out") before the tests that produced it.
- 2-1-3-4 fails because "a page or a monitor… such conditions" in 1 needs 3's page, monitor, seventy metres and rain.

*Audit note:* the blind re-audit flagged 2-1-3-4 as a weak rival. Sentence 1 was reworded to lock it to 3.

### Q19. Answer: 4132 (para jumble; easy)

**Correct order:**
1. **4** makes the claim: workers took the weekend for themselves.
2. **1** shows how: absence on Mondays.
3. **3** gives the employers' response to "the practice".
4. **2** concludes: "in other words", a bargain with a habit.

### Q20. Answer: D, gap 4 (sentence placement; difficult)

**What the sentence needs:** "What kills a reef, *in other words*, is less any single episode than *the shortening of the time between them*." It restates a point already made, about repeated episodes compounding.

| Gap | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1 | No episodes have been described yet. |
| 2 | Only the mechanism of bleaching has been given. |
| **3** | **Locally plausible.** The years of rebuilding have just been mentioned. But "in other words" must restate something, and the claim about the time between episodes hasn't been made yet. The next sentence makes it. Inserted here, the summary would come before what it summarises. |
| **4** | **Correct.** It summarises the preceding sentence: struck again before recovery, damage compounds. |

**Takeaway:** "in other words" restates something already said. It cannot introduce.

### Q21. Answer: C, gap 3 (sentence placement; difficult)

**What the sentence needs:** "Who should give up a seat, and on what terms, is a question that overbooking itself does not answer." It must follow the oversupply problem and come before the answers to it.

| Gap | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1 | Too early. No difficulty has been raised yet. |
| 2 | Comes before "The real difficulty arises…". The question would be posed before the situation that raises it. |
| **3** | **Correct.** Problem (more passengers than seats), then the question, then answer one (the airline chooses), then answer two (an auction). |
| **4** | **Locally plausible.** But it would re-pose the question after the paragraph has already answered it ("simply chose whom to remove"), which breaks the progression. |

### Q22. Answer: A (para summary; difficult)

**Why A is right:** it keeps the contrast: strongest for the few species successfully reintroduced, weakest for the big mammals that draw visitors.

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(D) Strongest trap.** It turns "a small *number of* species" into "small species". The paragraph's point is about how many species, not how big they are. "Succeeds only" also overstates "strongest for".
- **(B)** A prescription the paragraph never makes.
- **(C)** The paragraph never says zoos became easier to defend. It says the ark argument justifies "the zoos people actually visit" poorly.

### Q23. Answer: B (para summary; moderate)

**Why B is right:** it covers both the original function (preservation) and the reason the practice persists (taste valued for its own sake).

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(A) Wrong cause.** The paragraph says fermented foods stayed central "long after refrigeration made them unnecessary", so their survival is explained by *taste*, not by a preserving function that persists where refrigeration is scarce.
- **(C) Close trap.** "Habit" is not "taste". The paragraph says the flavours "came to be valued for their own sake".
- **(D)** Covers only the first half of the paragraph.

### Q24. Answer: A (para summary; moderate)

**Why A is right:** it keeps all three parts: the surveys measure something real, the long-run average is flat, and two readings remain that the data can't separate.

**Why the other options are wrong:**
- **(B)** Contradicts "measure something real".
- **(C)** Adopts one reading as established.
- **(D) Hedged, but it still takes sides.** "Probably conceal rising satisfaction" adopts the scale-shift reading. The paragraph says the data "cannot distinguish" between the readings, so even a hedged preference is unsupported.

**Takeaway:** when a paragraph ends by saying the evidence "cannot distinguish" between readings, the correct summary must not pick one of them.

## Diagnostic guide

| If you missed… | Work on |
|---|---|
| Q1, Q16 | Separating "was really about X" from "contained X without isolating it"; respecting the author's hedges |
| Q2, Q5 | Telling an assumption apart from a premise or fact used, and from a consideration the argument ignores |
| Q3, Q22 | Matching evidence or summaries to the *exact* claim (change over time; number vs size) |
| Q6, Q10 | Comparing qualifiers: suggests vs proves, not merely vs rather than, most vs only |
| Q11 | Weakening a "less X than Y" claim |
| Q20, Q21 | Using "in other words" and the problem → question → answer order in sentence placement |
